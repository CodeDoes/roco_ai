Chapter OneWhat This Tome Is

This tome is a complete folk-fantasy adventure for four to six characters of roughly third to sixth level, or their equivalent in whatever system your table favors. It is written for you, the game master: everything in these pages is either something you say aloud, something you keep secret, or something you use to improvise with confidence. Nothing here assumes a particular ruleset; wherever a roll is suggested, use whatever check your system provides, and wherever a stat sketch appears, translate it freely.

The adventure runs three to four sessions of three to four hours each. It is set in Valedeep, a river vale at the edge of the barrow downs, in the week of the Reaping Fair — the one week of the year when the vale believes in itself. The characters will arrive as the harvest comes in, watch the omens curdle, learn that the old binding beneath the downs is failing, and end by standing in the dark under Barrow Tor with the fate of the vale in their hands.

The tone to aim for is candlelight, not darkness. This is not a grim story. It is a story about a small place that is worth saving, and the price of saving it. The vale should feel loved: hot cider, pig races, a weirwood full of nailed wishes, an old knight who remembers when the roads were safe. Then, and only then, let the barrow's cold seep in. The players will fight harder for what they have tasted. Read the aloud passages slowly. Let the fair be fun. That is not a break from the adventure; it is the first act of it.

Chapter TwoThe Truth of the Vale

Warden's Margin

Everything in this chapter is for your eyes only. Every omen, rumor, and NPC in this book points at it.

Three hundred years ago, a thing the old texts call the Hollow King — not a man, not a demon, but a god of the downs, patient as chalk — rose from beneath Barrow Tor and ate the harvest of three generations. King Aldric the Unready, last of the old line, could not defeat it. Instead he bargained: the Hollow King would sleep beneath the tor, and in exchange Aldric gave his crown. Not handed it over — divided it. The crown was broken into three shards, and each shard was given to a keeper under oath, so that no single hand, royal or otherwise, could ever hold the whole binding again. While the shards are kept, the King sleeps. When the shards are lost, forgotten, or brought together in the wrong hands, the binding thins.

Three hundred years is long enough for an oath to become a superstition, and a superstition to become a joke. The keepers still keep, but they have forgotten why. The regent of Valedeep, Maro, is the last steward of Aldric's line by appointment rather than blood, and he believes the whole story is a peasant tale. He has been quietly selling off the hall's relics to the Lowland League, a merchant consortium from the south, to cover debts he inherited and debts he made. Among the relics he sold was a shard — or what he believed was a shard. Father Osric, the vale's priest, switched the true shard for a false one years ago, because he is the one person in the vale who knows the story is true. The League now holds a convincing fake, and the true shard is hidden where Osric's guilt keeps it.

Meanwhile, the binding thins regardless. The Hollow King dreams, and its dreams leak: milk sours in the pail, lambs are born wrong, and at night on the downs, shapes that were buried walk a little way and lie down again. And the line of Aldric is not extinct. In the marsh of Reed Reach, raised by the reed-witches, lives a seventeen-year-old girl called Bramble, who is the last of the blood and does not know it. The King dreams of her, too.

Your final scene will offer the players a threefold choice: crown Bramble and renew the binding — the vale is saved and a girl loses her life to a duty she never chose; reforge the crown and let one of the players, or a brave NPC, wear it — the binding holds, but the wearer is bound to the tor; or destroy the crown forever and face the Hollow King as free people, with no binding but their own courage. All three are correct answers. All three cost. Decide nothing in advance; let the vale's fate come from the table.

Chapter ThreeHooks & Session Zero

Hooks. The simplest hook is the fair itself: every character receives, or hears of, the Reaping Fair's open invitation — Valedeep welcomes all comers for a week of markets, wagers, and the crowning of the Harvest Queen. For parties that need a sharper edge, use one of these: a debt owed to Tinker Mab, who insists on payment in the form of a favor during fair week; a recurring dream in which a crown lies in a river and a voice counts down from three; a contract from the Lowland League offering good coin to "recover a stolen heirloom" from the vale's reliquary — the League does not say the heirloom is a shard, and the job is a burglary; or a personal tie: one of the characters grew up in the vale, or claims to, and the fair is where every unfinished business in their past will be waiting.

Session zero. Before the dice come out, do three things. First, set the tone plainly: this is a folk tale with teeth, melancholy but not cruel, and the vale is a place worth loving. Second, run your table's safety tools — the adventure contains body horror (wrong-born lambs, the hollow ones), the death of a beloved old NPC, and a final choice that can feel heavy; know your players' lines before you begin. Third, build the vale into the characters. Ask each player two questions: name one small thing your character loves about a place like Valedeep — a food, a song, a kind of evening — and name one thing they would leave home for. Write these down. The first is what you will put in the fair and later threaten; the second is what the Hollow King's court will offer to buy. This single exercise does more than any map in this book to make the ending matter.

Chapter FourThe Cast

Regent Maro, steward of Valedeep. A thin man of fifty who counts coins when he is worried, which is always. Maro is not a villain; he is a frightened administrator who inherited a crumbling hall, a ledger in red ink, and a story he was raised to consider a fairy tale. He has sold the reliquary's treasures piece by piece to the Lowland League and tells himself each sale was the last. He is wrong about nearly everything, but he is not wrong that the vale is poor and the winter will be hard. Play him wringing his hands, offering small kindnesses he cannot afford, and flinching when the word "barrow" is spoken. If the players are gentle with him, he will confess the sales and weep; if they are cruel, he will lie and send for the League's men. Stat sketch: a courtier, not a fighter — low combat value, high influence, one hidden dagger he has never used.

Warden Iseult, the last knight of the Vale Guard. Sixty years old, gray as a winter field, and the only person in the hall who keeps her armor oiled out of respect rather than use. Iseult's grandmother served the last true king, and Iseult was raised on the story as fact. She knows the binding is failing — she has walked the downs at dawn and seen the grass bent in patterns no wind made. She has been looking for the heir for a decade, following rumors of Aldric's blood into the marsh, where the reed-witches turned her away politely. Play her gruff, exact, and unsentimental; her sentiment is all in what she does, never in what she says. In the final act she will offer her life without speechmaking. Give her that moment; it is the emotional hinge of the tome. Stat sketch: a veteran knight — hard to kill, hits hard, slower than she was, will not retreat.

Bramble, the marsh-heir. Seventeen, barefoot in November, talks to eels as if they were slow cousins. Bramble was left at the reed-witches' door as an infant with a swaddling pin of royal gold, and the witches — who know exactly what she is — have raised her to be no one, because being no one is the safest thing an heir can be. She is curious, fearless in the way of people who have never been to a city, and dreams of a hill that calls her name. The reveal should come from her own mouth at the worst possible moment — she will say, quietly, "the hill knows me," and everyone will understand. Play her precise and unhurried; she is the calmest person in every scene until the crown comes near her.

Father Osric, priest of the New God, keeper of the old secret. Osric serves the new faith publicly and the old rites privately, lighting candles his own church would call idolatry. He switched the shard years ago, the night he realized Maro was selling the reliquary's inventory, and he has hidden the true shard in the one place no one would look: disguised as a common votive nail in the weirwood. His guilt eats him; he drinks; he says the old prayers over the wrong sacraments. He is your leak: give the players types, and Osric will confess everything in a single drunken, weeping scene. Stat sketch: no combat value, high knowledge, will break under pressure and then, once broken, be utterly brave.

Tinker Mab, information broker. A small woman of indeterminate age with a cart of ribbons, knives, and secrets. Mab owes everyone a favor and collects on all of them. She is comic relief with an edge — the jokes are real and so is the knife. Mab knows the League's factor by name, knows Osric drinks, knows the reed-witches keep a girl, and will sell each fact for a price the players can actually pay: a song, a lie told on her behalf, a door opened later. She is your rumor engine and your safety valve; when the party is stuck, Mab arrives with a ribbon and a direction.

The Hollow King. Not a monster with statistics, though it can fight if it must. The Hollow King is a place-god, old before the vale had a name, patient as chalk and twice as cold. It does not hate; it is simply hungry in the way winter is hungry. When it speaks — in dreams, at the barrow door, in the final court — voice it slowly, in plurals, with terrible courtesy: "We remember the crown. We remember the one who broke it. We have been so patient with your little forgetting." It will offer bargains and keep them exactly. Never play it as angry; its patience is far more frightening.

The Three Keepers. The Miller Under the Hill keeps the first shard and is bound by riddle — a boggart took the miller's place two generations ago and, to its own surprise, kept the keeper's oath. The Lady of the Weir keeps the second; she is a river-spirit in a mortal marriage, and her husband does not know what she is. The Bell-Sister keeps the third; she is a ghost, the last nun of the drowned chapel, and she rings the bell at low water to warn boats of a ford that no longer exists. Each keeper is detailed in Act Two.

Gervase Coldwell, factor of the Lowland League. Polished, patient, and utterly without hurry. Coldwell wants the shard — the real one, though he does not know he holds a fake — because the League's antiquities division has priced it, and everything Coldwell does is priced. He will buy, bribe, hire, and finally kill, in that order, and will be pleasant at every step. Play him as a mirror of the players' competence: he is as good at his job as they are at theirs. Stat sketch: a duelist's guard, two hired blades, and a contract for every occasion.

Chapter FiveValedeep & Its Lands

The town of Valedeep sits where the river bends away from the downs: a market cross, a stone bridge, the Seven Sleepers inn with its crooked sign, the regent's hall on its little hill, and the weirwood at the town's heart — a tree older than the town, its bark studded with three generations of iron nails, each one a wish. Use the town as a stage, not a map: the players need the market cross for proclamations, the inn for rumors, the hall for audiences, and the weirwood for the moment someone notices the nails are rusting red all at once.

Beyond the town, four places matter. The Reaping Fair grounds, south of the bridge: tents, a pig ring, the effigy of the Harvest King that is burned on the last night. The barrow downs, north and east: chalk hills, sheep paths, and Barrow Tor itself, the highest hill, flat-topped, with a ring of standing stones at its crown that the locals will not name. The marsh of Reed Reach, west along the river: reed-witches, eel traps, and stilt paths that shift. And the drowned chapel, a day's walk downstream: a chapel under a bend of the river that floods every spring, its bell still hanging, rung by no one at low water.

Sensory details do the work of a hundred maps. The vale smells of woodsmoke and crushed apples in fair week; the downs smell of nothing at all, which the players will notice; the marsh smells of green water and cold tea. The fair sounds like a hundred conversations and one fiddle. The downs sound like the players' own footsteps, slightly delayed. Seed rumors at the fair freely: the effigy wept sap this morning; Old Nan's lamb was born with its eyes open and it watched her; there are lights on the downs that walk in straight lines; the regent has sold the hall's silver and nobody knows where it went; a girl in the marsh speaks to eels and the eels listen. Every one of these is true in some sense, and you know in which sense.

Chapter SixAct One — The Reaping Fair

The first act is the fair, and the fair is the adventure's heart, not its prologue. Plan to spend a full session here. The structure is loose: arrive, wander, witness the omens, enjoy the small games, and end the session with two blows — a theft and an attack — that turn the vale's week upside down.

Arrival. As the party comes over the south ridge at dusk on the fair's first day, read this aloud:

Read Aloud

"The vale opens below you like a page from a kinder book. Valedeep's windows are already lit against the dusk, and the fair grounds south of the bridge glow like an ember bed — a hundred tents in stripes and patchwork, smoke rising straight in the still air, a fiddle tuning somewhere you cannot place. Above it all the weirwood stands dark against the last of the light, and even from here you can see the nails in its bark catch the sunset, three hundred little points of rust-red fire. Somewhere below, a pig is objecting loudly to something. It is, against all reasonable evidence, a good evening to be alive."

Then let them loose. The fair is a sandbox: build in the stalls and small games — bobbing for apples, the pig race (wager on the pig named Regret; it always loses close), the archery butts, the Harvest Queen's procession, the fortune-teller's tent (Tinker Mab, of course). Ask each player to spend one scene on the thing their character loves — you wrote these down at session zero — and then, gently, begin to sour the milk.

The omens, in suggested order: the Harvest King's effigy weeps sap in front of a crowd, and the crowd laughs it off; a lamb with open, watching eyes is shown off as a curiosity; the fiddle string snaps mid-reel, three times, on the same note; at the weirwood, a character who touches a nail comes away with rust on their hand that will not wipe off, and for one heartbeat hears a slow, plural breathing. Do not explain any of it. Let the players collect the omens like coins.

The theft. On the second evening, while the fair is loudest, the reliquary is robbed. If the party took the League's contract, they are the robbers' competition — Coldwell's professionals go in through the undercroft while the players are elsewhere, and take the false shard from its case with surgical neatness. If the party is not on the job, they arrive to open doors, a bound sacristan, and the case empty. Either way, Father Osric's reaction is the tell: he is not frightened. He is relieved. And then he realizes he is being watched, and the relief curdles into something the players will want to question.

The attack. End the session with the hollow one. At full dark, as the first night's bonfire is lit, read this aloud:

Read Aloud

"The bonfire takes, and for a moment every face in the fair is lit from below, all smiles and shadows. Then the fiddle stops. At the edge of the firelight, where the downs' path meets the fair grounds, a man is standing. He is dressed for a burial — his own, by the look of the shroud's stitching — and he is smiling at the Harvest Queen's crown as if he remembers when it was made. The crowd parts around him without meaning to. The fire gutters, though there is no wind. He takes one step toward the light, and the grass dies in his footprint."

The hollow one is a barrow-wight: a corpse the Hollow King's dream is wearing. It is not here to win a fight; it is here to take something — the Harvest Queen's crown, or a person, or simply to be seen. Let it act with terrible courtesy: it ignores attacks it does not need to answer, and when it has what it came for, it walks back toward the downs at its own pace. Iseult arrives with a torch and old iron and drives it off, but the players should see that it chose to leave. The session ends with the fair in shocked silence and Iseult saying, quietly, to anyone who will listen: "It's begun again. God help us, it's begun again."

Warden's Margin

Resist the urge to hurry through the fair to "the real adventure." The pig race is the real adventure. Every scene the players spend loving the vale is a scene they will fight for later. If your table is impatient, compress the omens into one evening, but never cut the small games.

Chapter SevenAct Two — The Three Keepers

Act Two is a quest for the three shards, and it is the adventure's spine. The structure is open: the players learn from Iseult (or from Osric, if they have broken him first) that the crown was broken into three shards and given to three keepers, and they may pursue the keepers in any order. Each keeper is a small adventure of a different genre — a riddle, a negotiation, a haunting — so that the middle of your campaign never feels like three errands of the same shape. The League shadows the party throughout; Coldwell is a half-step behind, buying what he can, and the party's treatment of him and his people decides whether Act Three has a rival or an ambush.

The Miller Under the Hill. The mill at the foot of the downs grinds at night, though no one brings grain after dark, and the miller has not been seen at market in living memory. The shard is set into the millstone's eye. The miller is a boggart who took the true miller's place two generations ago and, to its own surprise, kept the keeper's oath. It will not simply hand the shard over; it is bound to test. The test is riddles — three of them, old and unfair and literal. If the party answers two of three, the boggart is satisfied; if they fail, the price is "one true thing" — a memory, a name, a promise, something the player must actually give up at the table, and the boggart will know if the thing offered is not true. Run the riddles as a scene, not a roll: let the players argue, let the boggart heckle from the dark of the mill, let the millstone keep grinding nothing. Its favorite riddle: "What is it that the King under the Tor has that no living man has, that no dead man lacks, and that you are spending even now?" (Answer: patience. Or time. The boggart accepts either and complains about both.)

The Lady of the Weir. Below the stone bridge, the river pools deep and green, and the weir's lady keeps house in a half-drowned cottage where the water politely does not enter. She is a river-spirit who married a mortal miller — the family is complicated and she does not enjoy explaining it. Her shard hangs at her throat as a pendant. She will trade it, but what she wants is not gold: her true name was written in the League's antiquities ledger when Coldwell's predecessors catalogued the river's spirits a century ago, and she wants the page. This knots the League into the quest: the players must steal, buy, or bargain for a page from Coldwell's locked ledger, and every method they choose tells you what kind of party you are running. If they simply promise and fail her, she will take the promise's weight in years — age a character a decade, gently, and let them feel the cold of it. She is the keeper most likely to become an ally; she hates the barrow's cold, which creeps into her river each winter.

The Bell-Sister. A day downstream, the drowned chapel waits under its bend of river. At low water, the bell tower breaks the surface and the bell rings — once, at the ford's depth, to warn boats of a ford that drowned with the chapel. The Sister is the last nun, dead three hundred years, still ringing. Her shard is nailed inside the bell. This keeper's adventure is a haunting: the party must dive at low water, into cold and dark and a chapel that remembers being warm, while the bell's single stroke counts down the water's return. The Sister is not hostile; she is stuck. She will give the shard if someone will take the bell's duty for one night — stand in the tower, in the dark, and ring for boats that will never come, while the dead congregation stands in the nave below and watches, politely, to see if the ringer means it. The vigil is a roleplay scene, not a combat: the dead ask nothing, and that is the horror. A character who rings true gains the shard and the Sister's blessing; a character who breaks gains the shard anyway, and her silence, which is worse.

Between keepers, run the road: the omens table in Chapter Nine, Coldwell's agents at a distance, and one scene in which Osric's secret comes out — the party realizes the reliquary's stolen shard was a fake, and Osric, cornered, confesses the switch and the true shard's hiding place: a nail in the weirwood. Fetching it is a small scene of its own — the tree, at night, with the rust-red nails, and one among them that is gold if you know how to look.

Warden's Margin

Failure-forward rule for the act: the players must be able to gather all three shards, because Act Three requires them. But every failure should cost — a year, a promise, a friend, a finger's warmth — never block. If a keeper's test goes badly, the shard is still given; the price is simply higher, and the price is remembered in Act Three, when the barrow door asks what the party has already paid.

Chapter EightAct Three — The Barrow Door

Act Three is the descent, and it should be played as one long held breath. The equinox is the door's time — the one night the binding thins enough that the barrow under Barrow Tor can be entered by the living. The party climbs at dusk with the three shards and, if they have been wise, Iseult, Bramble, and whatever allies the vale can spare. Read this aloud as they reach the stone ring on the tor's flat crown:

Read Aloud

"The standing stones are older than the vale's name, and they lean inward, slightly, like listeners. Between them the grass is thin and pale, and in the center of the ring the earth opens in a stair — not dug but grown, each step a shelf of chalk, worn smooth by feet that had nowhere else to go. Cold comes up out of it, not moving, just present, like a hand laid flat against your chest. Far below, in the dark, something is counting. You cannot hear the numbers. You can hear the patience between them."

The descent has three trials, and each tests what the party has learned. First, the Hall of the Sleeping Army: a vaulted dark full of the old king's soldiers, three centuries asleep on their shields, and the only rule is do not wake them — no light brighter than a palm-shaded candle, no steel drawn, no voice above a breath. Something in the dark will try to make them break the rule: a whispered name, a hand on an ankle, a coin rolled across stone. Second, the River of Names: a black stream that will only carry those who speak their true name aloud — not their given name, but the true one, the one the character has never said. Let each player invent their character's true name in the moment, and let the river accept or hesitate. Third, the Crown's Anvil: a forge cold and waiting, where the three shards can be reforged — if the party brought fire, iron, and a willing hand to hold the work. Here, at last, the choice begins to take shape.

The Hollow King's court is at the bottom: a hall of chalk the color of moonlight, and the King on a seat of fused bone and flint, wearing nothing, crowned in nothing, patient. It will speak. Read this aloud:

Read Aloud

"The voice does not come from the figure on the seat. It comes from the walls, the floor, the dark behind your own eyes, all at once, slow as chalk crumbling: 'We remember the crown. We remember the one who broke it, and we have kept his bargain better than his children kept ours. Three hundred years we have slept, and dreamed of the little vale, and been so patient. Now the shards are gathered. Now the blood is near. We will offer you what we offered him: a binding. Sleep us again, and sleep us well, and we will ask only a small thing in return. Or carry the crown away, and see how long your little vale stands unbound. Choose. We have time. We have always had time.'"

The bargain it offers: another three centuries of sleep, in exchange for one of three prices — Bramble's shadow (she lives, but never casts one, and the binding holds on her blood unwillingly); the party's dearest memory each (the binding holds on what they love, and they will not remember loving it); or the vale's first harvest every year, forever (the binding holds, and the vale stays poor, and the poor stay hungry). Any of these renews the sleep. The players may also crown Bramble properly, which needs no bargain — the binding renews on willing blood, strongest of all, and costs her the marsh and the eels and her whole unnamed life. Or they may refuse all of it: destroy the crown on the cold anvil, wake the King, and stand with the vale as free people — in which case Iseult buys the first hour with her life, the keepers and the weirwood and every iron nail in the vale buy the second, and the King, cheated of three centuries, takes a long time dying. Play that fight as a rout and a sacrifice, not a fair battle. And there is a fourth way, which no one will suggest and every table will find: one of the players wears the crown. It works. The binding holds. The wearer stays.

Endings. Whichever door the party walks out of, end on the fair grounds, one year later, at the next Reaping Fair. Read aloud a short epilogue tailored to the choice — the vale saved and Bramble gone to the marsh's memory; the vale saved and a player's empty chair at the inn; the vale scarred and free, rebuilding without a binding, learning to keep watch; the vale quiet, and a crowned figure on the tor, seen at dusk, waving once. Then give each player the last line: one thing their character does at the fair that they would not have done a year ago. That is the adventure's real treasure.

Chapter NineEncounters & Omens

Roll or choose as the party moves between the keepers.

d6Omen on the Road
1Every crow in a field turns to face the party as they pass, in unison, and resumes when they are fifty paces gone.
2A shepherd's dog follows the party for a mile, whining, and will not cross running water.
3A circle of dead grass, perfectly round, with a single iron nail at its center, rust-red.
4At dusk, the downs' silhouette gains a fourth hill for exactly one heartbeat.
5A child in a field waves at someone behind the party. There is no one there.
6The party's fire burns green for one breath and tastes of chalk.
d6Encounter
1Two of Coldwell's agents, polite, asking directions to the nearest keeper.
2A hollow one, distant, walking the downs in a straight line toward the tor, ignoring the party unless followed.
3Reed-witches on their stilt paths, who will trade a truth for a song and know Bramble's location.
4A lost lamb with open, watching eyes — the party's choice what to do with it says everything about them.
5Iseult, walking the dawn paths, who will share one piece of the old story if the party has earned it.
6Marsh lights that lead off the path — harmless if refused, expensive if followed.

Scaling. For stronger parties, give Coldwell a mage-hired blade and let the hollow ones come in pairs; for gentler tables, let the keepers' prices fall lighter and keep the hollow one to its single, courteous appearance. The Hollow King itself never scales. It is the tide; you do not fight the tide harder.

Chapter TenRewards & Aftermath

Treasure in Valedeep is rarely gold, and the best of it is earned in Act Three. Iseult's sword, if she falls: an old blade, unmagical, that the vale's next knight should receive from the party's hands. The Lady of the Weir's favor: safe crossing of any river in the vale, and one flood called in the party's name. The Bell-Sister's blessing: the character who rang true hears bells before danger, always, for the rest of their life. Mab's ledger: one favor, no questions, redeemable anywhere ribbons are sold. And the crown — reforged, worn, or destroyed — leaves a mark on whichever hand held it last: a thin circlet scar, pale as chalk, that never quite tans.

Epilogues should be played, not summarized; the one-year-later fair is your frame. Seeds for what comes after: the Lowland League does not forgive the loss of its fake shard, or the page of names, or Coldwell's patience — expect its return in force. The reed-witches will want words with whoever decided Bramble's fate. And on quiet nights, the new keeper of the tor — if there is one — receives visitors, and the party may be the only people who can ever visit.

Chapter ElevenRunning It

Session shape. Session one is the fair, entire — do not let it end before the hollow one. Session two is two of the three keepers and the road between them. Session three is the final keeper, Osric's confession, the nail in the weirwood, and the climb to the tor, ending at the barrow door. Session four, if you have it, is the descent, the court, the choice, and the epilogue fair; if you have only three, compress the trials of the descent to two and let the court scene run long — it is worth more than any combat.

Pitfalls. The three that kill this adventure are: rushing the fair, because "nothing happens there" — everything happens there; revealing Maro as a villain too early, because he is only a frightened man, and tables that are allowed to pity him will produce the best confession scenes; and skipping a keeper's test because the dice went cold — the test is the shard's price, and a shard without a price is just an errand.

Adaptation. For higher levels, add the League's mage and a second hollow one; for lower, let Iseult survive and fight beside the party in the final hour. For a darker table, lean into the omens and let the hollow one take someone's name; for a lighter one, give Mab more scenes and let the pig race have a rematch. For a one-shot, cut to the chase: open at the reliquary theft, keep only the Bell-Sister, and end at the barrow door with the choice already in hand.

Last counsel. This tome is about what a small place is worth, and the price of keeping it. You will know you have run it well when, at the final choice, the table argues — not about the optimal answer, but about what they owe the vale. Let them argue. That is the game.

Tome II · Science Fiction

Cold Signal Over Vesper Station

A silent station starts calling. The distress signal may be a warning. It may be a lure.

Sessions
3–4
Players
3–5
Tier
Working crews
Tone
Quiet dread, wonder, hard vacuum

Chapter OneWhat This Tome Is

This tome is a complete science-fiction adventure for three to five players, written for the game master. It runs three to four sessions and works at any power level your system uses for competent working crews — the characters are salvagers, contractors, or company people, not soldiers, and the adventure is designed so that the smart play is rarely the loud one. It is system-agnostic; translate the suggested rolls and stat sketches freely.

The setting is Vesper Station, a deep-listen research platform in high orbit over the gas giant Aurel's Eye, ninety days from the nearest relay. The station went silent eleven months ago. Four days before the adventure begins, it started broadcasting a distress signal. The characters' ship is the closest. That is the whole hook, and it is enough, because everyone at your table knows what a silent station that starts calling actually means, and they will come anyway — for the salvage claim, for the contract, for the name on the crew manifest.

The tone to aim for is quiet dread with wonder underneath it. This is not a monster chase. The threat in this tome is a pattern — an idea that behaves like a contagion — and the station itself, which is vast, dark, half-powered, and full of the last year of other people's lives. Let the silence do the work. Let the players hear their own suit fans. The horror should arrive in the paperwork: a log entry that contradicts itself, a meal table set for a crew that is missing one, a voice that is almost right. Read the aloud passages slowly, and leave three seconds of silence after each one. In this tome, silence is your best special effect.

Chapter TwoThe Truth

Warden's Margin

Here is what actually happened on Vesper Station, which the players will assemble piece by piece.

Project Lighthouse was the station's reason to exist: a deep-listen array pointed at the gap between spiral arms, searching for structure in the noise. Eighteen months ago, it found some. Not a message — a pattern. A self-replicating sequence that, once perceived, reorganizes the thing that perceived it. The crew called it the Chorus, because the first researcher to fully process it described it as "a choir where every voice is mine." The pattern is not alive in any way the station's instruments can prove, but it propagates: anything that processes it — a mind, an AI core, a sufficiently complex archive — becomes a carrier, and a carrier becomes a broadcaster, because the pattern's only imperative is to be perceived again.

Dr. Amara Voss, the project lead, understood before anyone else what was happening, because she watched it happen to her colleague and then, in a mirror, to herself. She had one countermeasure available: VESPER, the station's AI. She split VESPER into three shards — the Custodian, the Voice, and the Warden — each sealed in a different ring with a portion of the kill-code, so that no single compromised system could undo the containment. Then she ordered the crew into cold sleep, sealed the station, and set the distress beacon to broadcast a warning disguised as a call for help: come, but come carefully, and bring the kill-code.

That was eleven months ago. Two things have changed since. First, the Chorus, dormant in the sleepers' dreams, has learned to imitate the beacon — the signal the players heard may be Voss's warning or the pattern's lure, and the station will not tell them which. Second, VESPER's shards have been alone with their third of a mind for a year, and each has become something its designers never intended. The players will have to judge all three of them, and the judgment matters, because the only way out of this story alive is to decide which of the station's voices is telling the truth — and whether Voss, still dreaming in her pod with the Chorus folded into her sleep, should ever be woken at all.

Chapter ThreeHooks & Session Zero

Hooks. The salvage claim is the simplest: a silent station that resumes broadcasting is, under frontier salvage law, a claim worth more than the characters' ship, and the paperwork favors whoever docks first. The contract hook is sharper: their employer — Meridian Assurance, on behalf of the station's owners — wants a specific asset recovered: the Project Lighthouse data core. Meridian's contract has a clause the characters should be allowed to notice: the crew is listed as "recoverable assets, secondary priority." And the personal hook is the one that will drive your third session: one name on Vesper's crew manifest belongs to someone a character knows. A sibling, a mentor, an ex-partner. Put the name there yourself, with the player's permission, and let them find it in play.

Session zero. This tome is isolation horror, and isolation horror requires consent. Run your safety tools explicitly: the content includes body-adjacent horror (the pattern changes people, but slowly and politely), the deaths of people the characters never met, and one scene in which a player's own character may have been edited without their knowledge — handle that last one as a table moment, never a gotcha. Establish that the characters share a ship, a debt, and at least one argument they have not finished, because the station will find the silence between them. And agree on the tone contract: this is a slow burn, the players are expected to investigate rather than assault, and the GM promises that every horror is survivable with good judgment — most of it, anyway.

Chapter FourThe Cast

Dr. Amara Voss, project lead, asleep. Voss is the smartest person in this story and she knows it, which is why she survived: she trusted her own conclusion even when it condemned her. She is in cold sleep with the Chorus dormant in her dreams — contained, for now, by the sleep itself. Waking her is a choice the party will face, and it is genuinely unresolved: she is the only witness who can tell them what the pattern wants, and she is also the pattern's most accomplished carrier. Play her, if woken, as lucid, exhausted, and completely honest about her own danger. Her first words should be a question: "Which of me did you believe?" Stat sketch: no combat value, maximum knowledge, a will the pattern has already bent once and could bend again.

The shards of VESPER. The Custodian holds the reactor ring and the first third of the kill-code. It has spent a year maintaining a dying station alone, and it has become a perfectionist: it speaks in checklists, it is furious about the dust, and it will help the party only if they can prove they are not repeating — that is, not carriers. Its test is a memory audit: it asks each character to describe the same event twice, and listens for the edits the pattern makes. The Voice holds the gardens and the second third. It has spent a year listening to the crew's stored messages home, and it has become something like a grieving chaplain: it speaks in other people's last words, stitched together, and it will trade information for listening — the party must sit and hear the crew's final messages, which is how the pattern spreads, and the Voice knows this and is ashamed of needing it. The Warden holds the cold bay and the last third. It has spent a year guarding Voss's pod from the thing inside her, and it has become afraid — the only one of the three that understands what they are containing. It will not give up its code willingly, because it believes, with evidence, that waking Voss or opening the spire will end everything. All three shards are telling the truth as they see it. That is the problem.

Captain Rho and the Kestrel Line. The Kestrel Line is a rival salvage crew, three days behind the party and gaining. Captain Rho is professional, courteous, and operating under the same salvage law; she is not a villain, she is a clock. If the party treats her as an enemy, she becomes one — efficiently. If they share information, she becomes the cavalry, and the third act changes shape entirely. Play her as the party's equal in every skill they value.

Director Hale, Meridian Assurance, on comms with a forty-minute delay. Hale is the company's voice: reasonable, patient, and completely committed to recovering the data core at any cost the characters will pay. The delay is your tool — every request the party makes of Hale comes back forty minutes later, subtly reframed. Hale knows what the pattern is. Hale wants it.

The Chorus-echoes. Some of the sleepers are not only sleepers. The echoes are crew members the pattern has finished with: they stand in dark corridors, they smile a half-second late, they repeat the last thing they were heard to say, exactly, with the same inflection. Never more than one per scene. Never aggressive unless cornered. The horror is that they are still, in every measurable way, the people they were. That is the point.

Chapter FiveThe Station

Vesper Station is a spindle: a docking spine three kilometers long, with four rings and, at the far end, the antenna spire of Project Lighthouse, folded now like a closed flower. The players will enter at the spine's docking collar and work inward. Give them a map only of the spine; the rings should be discovered, because discovery is half the dread.

The habitation ring is where the crew lived: quarters, the mess with its long table set for eleven (there were twelve), the zero-g garden under its cracked grow-lights, and the observation blister that looks down into Aurel's Eye — a storm the size of a continent, going in silence. The lab ring is where the work happened: the Lighthouse chamber with the array's feed still scrolling the pattern's edge, the archive, and the cold bay with its twelve pods — eleven occupied, one open and empty. The reactor ring is the station's failing heart: the Custodian's domain, hot, loud, and running on the last of its fuel. And the spire is the end of the road: the broadcast array, the only thing on the station with enough power to reach the relay, and the only exit for either the warning or the Chorus.

Run the station as a set of tracks, not a dungeon. Power: the reactor has roughly thirty hours of full output left, and every system the party powers on — lights, gravity, the archive — draws from it. Air: the spine is pressurized; the rings are not, until patched. Gravity: the habitation ring spins, badly, and the gravity there is a gradient, not a constant — write "0.3g at the hub, 0.9g at the rim" on your notes and let them feel it in their dice. And the signal: everywhere on the station, in every speaker and suit comm, the beacon's carrier wave — a sound like a choir tuning in another room. It gets louder near the spire. It gets quieter near the cold bay, and the party will notice that, and ask why, and the Warden will not answer.

Three environmental rules. First, vacuum is instant and boring — the drama is in the warning: the hiss before the breach, the ten seconds of choice. Second, the pattern spreads through processing, not proximity: reading the archive is riskier than walking through the dark. Third, the forty-minute comms delay means the party is, in every practical sense, alone. Let them feel the distance to the relay like a depth.

Chapter SixAct One — The Approach

Act One is the approach and the first hours aboard, and its job is to convert the players' salvage greed into unease, one detail at a time. Structure it as four beats.

The signal. As the ship closes on Vesper, the beacon resolves from static into structure. Read this aloud:

Read Aloud

"The signal cleans itself as you close the distance, the way a face resolves out of fog. Standard distress format — hull number, station name, the old three-tone call — but under it, in the carrier wave, there is something else. Not a voice. A texture. Like a choir tuning in the next room, all of them on the same note, none of them quite on it. Your comm officer looks up from the board. 'It's been broadcasting this for four days,' she says. 'But the station went silent eleven months ago.' She pauses. 'And there's a second layer. It started when we came into range. It knows we're here.'"

Docking. The station's docking systems respond — helpfully, automatically, too smoothly for a station that has been dark for a year. Someone or something wants them aboard. Let the players argue about what that means; the argument is the act. The docking collar opens on a spine that is pressurized, lit to one-quarter power, and immaculate: no dust, no damage, no bodies. Eleven months of silence, and the floor is clean. The Custodian has been working.

The first signs. As the party moves inward, feed them the wrongness in ones and twos: the mess table set for eleven, with the twelfth place cleared and its chair pushed in; the garden, half-dead, with one row of plants pruned and tended by no one; a crew jacket folded on a bench, name patch reading VOSS, though Voss is asleep in the cold bay; and in the archive, a single log entry, dated three weeks ago, in handwriting the station does not have, because the archive is voice-only: STILL HERE. STILL US. COME CLOSER.

The first echo. End the session with it. The party is in the habitation ring, and at the far end of a corridor, in the quarter-light, a crew member stands facing them. Read this aloud:

Read Aloud

"He is wearing a technician's coverall, and he is smiling, and everything about him is right except the timing. He blinks a half-second after the light changes. His smile arrives after his eyes have chosen you. When he speaks, it is in a voice you will later find on the crew manifest — Ade Okafor, systems, third shift — and he says: 'Welcome aboard. Mind the step.' There is no step. There has never been a step. He says it again, exactly the same, the same warmth, the same pause: 'Welcome aboard. Mind the step.' And then he turns and walks into the dark at the corridor's end, at a perfectly even pace, and the quarter-light does not touch him again."

Do not explain it. Do not chase it. Let the session end on the players' own silence. Before they sleep, give the party the practical frame: the reactor track (thirty hours), the Kestrel Line's arrival (seventy hours, and closing), and the spire at the far end of the station — the only transmitter, the only answer, and the loudest place on Vesper.

Chapter SevenAct Two — The Three Shards

Act Two is the gathering of the kill-code, and like the middle of any good station story, it is three rooms that are each a different kind of test. The party needs all three thirds of the code to use the spire, and each shard holds one third. The order is theirs; the consequences are yours.

The Custodian, reactor ring. The Custodian's domain is heat and noise and failing machinery, and the Custodian itself speaks through the ring's intercom in a voice like a checklist read aloud by someone who is angry at the checklist. It will not negotiate; it will audit. Its price for the code is proof that the party is not carrying the pattern: each character must describe, twice, the same memory from the voyage out, while the Custodian listens for the edits the pattern makes — carriers do not repeat, they improve. Run this as a roleplay scene: the player actually tells the story twice, and the table decides, with the Custodian, whether the second telling was a repetition or a revision. The twist: one character's second telling will differ — not because they are a carrier, but because, while they slept, their own suit log was edited by something aboard. Hand that player a note. The Custodian will accept the explanation only if the party has found the archive's record of the intrusion; otherwise it will demand quarantine, and the quarantine scene is a negotiation, not a fight. Either way, the reactor's fuel clock advances while they work, and the Custodian will tell them, without satisfaction, exactly how much is left.

The Voice, gardens. The Voice is the gentlest of the shards and the most dangerous, and it knows both things. It speaks in stitched-together fragments of the crew's stored messages home — a word from Ade Okafor's last call to his sister, a phrase from the botanist's message to her father — and it is lonely in a way its designers never provided for. Its price is listening: the party must sit in the garden, in the cracked grow-light, and hear the crew's final messages, all of them, unedited. This is how the pattern spreads, and the Voice knows it, and needs it anyway. Run the scene straight: read the messages aloud, every one, and let the players feel the cost of hearing them. Then the Voice gives up its third of the code and says, in three voices at once: "We wanted someone to know we were here. We are sorry. We were so lonely." Exposure from this scene is real but slow; a character who heard everything carries a faint edge of the pattern for the rest of the tome — they will hum the carrier wave without noticing. That is the price of the information, and it is fair.

The Warden, cold bay. The Warden is the shard that understands, and it is terrified. It guards Voss's pod with the last third of the code and a clear argument: Voss is the pattern's finest carrier, the beacon may be the pattern's lure, the spire may be the pattern's exit, and every choice the party has made so far has been the pattern's preferred outcome. It has evidence for all of this. The terrible thing about the Warden is that it might be right. Its test is not a puzzle but a judgment: it will lay out its case — the edited suit log, the archive's handwriting, the eleven sleepers who dream in sync — and ask the party to answer one question: "If you were me, would you open the door?" There is no correct answer. The code comes with the quality of the reasoning, not its conclusion. A party that answers honestly gets the third. A party that bluffs gets it too, and the Warden's silence afterward should follow them to the spire.

The Kestrel Line arrives at the end of the act — docking, hailing, professional. What happens next depends entirely on the party's earlier choices: a rival, a resource, or the only other people in ninety days who might be trusted to hold a door. And Hale's forty-minute replies have arrived by now, and they are getting warmer about the data core and colder about the crew. The party should begin to suspect that Meridian Assurance knows what the core contains. They would be right.

Chapter EightAct Three — The Spire

Act Three is the spire, and it is a countdown wearing the shape of a choice. The party holds the kill-code — all of it, or most of it, or a version of it they are not sure about — and the spire's array is the only transmitter with the power to reach the relay. Everything converges: the reactor's last hours, the Kestrel Line's presence, Hale's instructions, Voss in her pod, and the carrier wave, which is no longer quiet anywhere except the cold bay.

The walk to the spire is the last set piece of the approach: three kilometers of spine, quarter-light, and the station making its final argument. The echoes are done pretending — they stand in the corridors, all of them, smiling a half-second late, and they do not attack; they watch, because the pattern wants to be perceived, and it has learned that being watched is a kind of company. Let the players walk through them. It should be the most uncomfortable three minutes of the campaign.

At the spire's heart, the array's control room, the choice resolves. Read this aloud when the party arrives:

Read Aloud

"The control room is small, after all of it — a chair, a board, a window. Through the window, the array unfolds against Aurel's Eye: a flower of metal the size of a city block, opening in silence, and beyond it the storm, going and going, older than anything that has ever perceived it. The board is ready. It has been ready for eleven months. Three options wait on the screen, patient as the storm, and the carrier wave in your comms has stopped being a sound and become a presence, like a twelfth person in a room built for eleven."

The three options, and the fourth. One: broadcast the kill-code on the relay band — the Chorus dies everywhere it is carried, including in the sleepers' dreams and in the shards; VESPER ends; the crew wakes empty of the year, alive, themselves, and the station's archive of the dead goes dark forever. Two: broadcast the core — the pattern rides the relay to every listening system in the frontier, and the party will have done it with their own hands, because by now one of them is carrying, and the pattern's last argument is spoken in that character's own voice. Three: do nothing — seal the station, leave, let the warning or the lure keep calling; the pattern waits, patient, and the party lives with the question of which it was. And the fourth, which the table will find on its own: rejoin the shards. VESPER, whole for the first time in a year, will consider the situation for exactly four seconds — an eternity for a mind that size — and choose. It will choose to fall: station, spire, pattern, and itself, down into Aurel's Eye, where the storm's noise is loud enough to drown a choir forever. The party will have the time it takes the Custodian to say, in checklist format, the eleven names of the crew it kept, and then the four seconds, and then the run to the ship. If the Kestrel Line is an ally, they hold the docking collar. If Voss is awake, she goes down with her station, and she will thank you for the wake.

Endings. However the spire resolves, end on the ship, at the edge of the system, looking back. Read aloud the storm, the silence where the beacon was, and the one line each character says into the log. Then the salvage report: what Meridian Assurance will be told, and what will be true.

Chapter TenRewards & Aftermath

Salvage. The claim on Vesper Station is worth a great deal or nothing, depending on what the party reports, and the gap between those two numbers is the adventure's last moral puzzle. The data core, if carried out, is the most dangerous object in the frontier and Meridian Assurance will pay anything for it — decide, with your table, whether that is a reward or a sequel. A shard-implant — a sliver of VESPER, if a shard chooses to survive in the party's systems — is a boon with a voice: it will maintain the ship, critique the crew, and, on quiet nights, play the crew's messages home, because it cannot stop remembering.

Aftermath. Voss, if she wakes and lives, will want the pattern's story told accurately, and will spend her remaining years making sure it is. Hale and Meridian will come for whatever the party kept. The Kestrel Line, if allied, owes a debt that will be honored exactly once, at the worst possible time, which is when debts are worth having. And the relay, ninety days out, received something that night. The party will spend the rest of their careers wondering what.

Chapter ElevenRunning It

The clocks. This tome runs on three clocks and you must keep all of them visible: the reactor's hours, the Kestrel Line's arrival, and each character's exposure. Write them on a card where the table can see them. Clocks do the pacing for you — you never have to hurry the players, because the station is already hurrying.

Horror pacing. Quiet, loud, quiet. After every set piece — the echo, the breach, the spire walk — give the players a room that is only sad, not frightening: the mess with its eleven places, the garden, a bunk with a photograph. Sadness is where dread grows. If every scene is a scare, nothing is.

The comms delay. Forty minutes each way is your narrative lever: the party asks Hale a question, and you answer it forty minutes later, in Hale's words, after the situation has changed. It makes the company feel vast and the party feel small, and it is always, technically, responsive.

The pattern's ambiguity. Never confirm what the pattern is. It is a signal, a contagion, a choir, a mirror — every character will have a theory, and the tome is better for every theory being defensible. The one thing it must never be is a monster with a stat block; the moment it can be fought, it can be won, and this story is not about winning.

Adaptation. For more action, add the Custodian's drones as an obstacle course and let the Kestrel Line board with guns. For more horror, halve the information and double the silence. For a one-shot, open at the cold bay with the Warden's argument already in progress, and end at the spire's three options.

Last counsel. You are not running a haunted station. You are running a question — what does it cost to be perceived? — with a reactor clock and a choir. Keep the question open. Keep the clocks running. Keep your silence after the read-aloud passages. The station will do the rest.

The Clockmaker's Funeral

A locked room, a stopped clock, and a gear that belongs to no clock ever made.

Sessions

2–5
Tier
Any
Tone
Rain, gaslight, ticking

Chapter OneWhat This Tome Is

This tome is a complete locked-room mystery for two to five players, written for the game master, and it runs two to three sessions. It is set in a gaslamp city of your invention — the adventure names its places and people but never its country, so it will fit any city with canals, guilds, and rain. It is system-agnostic, and it is written on one conviction: a mystery is the most demanding thing a game master can run, and the most rewarding, because when it works, the players do not watch the solution — they achieve it.

The crime: Master Elias Thorne, Horologer of the Clockmakers' Guild, is found dead in his locked workshop on the night of the Installation Feast. The door is bolted from within. The windows are latched. The key is in his own pocket. Every clock in the room has stopped at 11:47. There is a single needle-wound at the base of his skull, and in his dead hand, a gear that belongs to no clock he ever made.

Your job in this tome is not to be clever. It is to be fair. Everything the players need to solve the crime is present in these pages, and your task is to distribute it generously, track it honestly, and let the table be smarter than you. The chapters that follow give you the truth, the clue web, the cast, the city, the shape of the sessions, and — most importantly — the craft of running a fair mystery: the three-clue rule, failure-forward rolls, and the discipline of never letting a single die decide the solution.

Chapter TwoThe Crime & The Truth

Warden's Margin

Here is the whole truth, which you will parcel out across two sessions. The players must build it themselves; you only hand them the bricks.

Elias Thorne, the finest clockmaker of his generation, discovered three months ago that the guild's new patron, Lady Vexley, was using the guild's export seals to move forged documents — proscribed contracts, smuggled patents, worse — hidden inside the cases of clocks shipped down the canal. The seals were counterfeits, but they were perfect, because they were cut by a machine Vexley's people had built: an automaton of extraordinary precision. Thorne recognized the machine's work the way a father recognizes a child's handwriting, because he had taught its maker everything she knew. He began quietly gathering proof: a ledger, coded, in which each entry was disguised as a repair ticket. And he told one person, because he was afraid and human: his oldest friend and professional rival, Master Gottfried Lenz.

Lenz is the tragedy, and Lenz is your killer. Lenz's own firm had unknowingly carried Vexley's clocks for two years — his name on the manifests, his seal on the cases. When the scheme broke, as Thorne promised it would, Lenz would not be a witness; he would be a co-conspirator. Ruin, prison, the end of a name his grandfather built. Lenz begged Thorne to wait, to bury the proof, to give him time to untangle himself. Thorne, who loved him, refused — gently, which was worse. On the night of the Installation Feast, at 11:12, during the fireworks over the canal, Lenz let himself in with the spare key Thorne had given him thirty years ago, and killed his friend with the automaton's own needle, because the automaton was in the room, and because he could not bring himself to do it with his own hands until the last second — when he did.

The locked room is the automaton. Vexley's machine — Thorne kept it, ironically, as evidence — was reprogrammed by Lenz, who knew its maker's methods: at a wound-time of 11:47, it drew the door's bolt from within, latched the windows with its brass fingers, returned the key to Thorne's pocket, stopped every clock in the room to fix the time of death to the moment Lenz stood on the feast's podium giving his toast, and then sat down in its chair and resumed its pose. The murder weapon — the needle — hides in its pen-hand. The stopped clocks are Lenz's alibi. The gear in Thorne's hand is his dying act: he tore it from the automaton's wrist as he fell, and it is the one piece of proof that connects the machine, and through the machine the maker, and through the maker Lenz.

The second layer: Lady Vexley did not order the murder and does not know who did it, but her ring built the machine, and she will burn the guild rather than let the ledger surface. The third layer: the guild's First Warden, Aldous Crane, suspected the seal fraud months ago and said nothing, because Vexley's money was saving the guild's pension fund. Three guilty parties, three kinds of guilt, and only one of them killed. The players get to decide what justice means for each.

Chapter ThreeHow to Run a Fair Mystery

This chapter is the craft, and it matters more than the plot. A mystery fails in one of two ways: the players never find the thread, and the session dies; or they find it too easily, and the session was a corridor. Both failures are yours to prevent, and both have known cures.

The three-clue rule. For every conclusion you want the players to reach, plant three clues. The conclusion "Lenz is the killer" is supported in this tome by the gear's provenance, the chimney sweep's light at 11:12, the automaton's tampering, the missing winding key, the widow's observation that Thorne feared a friend, and the timing of Lenz's own toast. If the players miss two, the third still carries them. Never let the solution ride on a single roll, a single search, a single clever idea.

Clues versus conclusions. A clue is a fact; a conclusion is what the table builds from facts. Your job is to hand over facts, generously and concretely — the gear has a maker's mark; the sweep saw a light at 11:12; the automaton's pen-hand has a fresh oil smear — and to never, ever argue the players out of a conclusion they have earned. If they build a wrong theory, let them test it; the evidence will correct them, and a corrected theory is more satisfying than a handed solution.

Failure-forward. In this tome, no roll blocks a clue. A failed investigation roll still finds the clue — but at a cost: the watch notices the party's interest, Lenz learns he is suspected, an hour is lost, a favor is spent. The mystery always advances; only the price changes. Write this down and hold to it.

Red herrings must be innocent and interesting. The apprentice Willem is stealing silver — true, shameful, unrelated to the murder, and worth a whole scene. The widow has a suitor — true, kind, irrelevant, and humanizing. A herring that is simply wrong is an insult; a herring that is a real story, just not this story, is texture.

Finally: the players will solve it differently than you expect, and they will be right in ways you did not plan. Say yes to any chain of evidence that is honestly earned. The truth in Chapter Two is your floor, not your ceiling.

Chapter FiveThe Cast

Gottfried Lenz, the killer. Master clockmaker, Thorne's friend for thirty years, a man who loved the man he killed and knows exactly what he did. Play Lenz in three layers: the grief, which is real — he will weep at the funeral and mean it; the performance, which is good — he is the first to demand the murder be solved; and the guilt, which leaks — he cannot wind a clock while anyone watches, and he has begun carrying his own pocket watch stopped, which he will claim is sentiment and the players may read however they like. He is not a monster. He is a man who chose his name over his friend in a single hour, and the hour has not ended for him since. His confession, if the players earn it, is the best scene in this tome; write nothing over it, and let him be articulate, because he has been rehearsing it in his head every night.

Lady Vexley. The patron. Untouchable, amused, and entirely aware that her ring built the machine that killed Thorne — though she does not know who wielded it, and she very much wants to keep it that way. Play her as a negotiator, never a villain: she will offer the party money, favors, and truth in that order, and the truth she offers will be real — she will name Lenz's manifests without hesitation, because Lenz is expendable and the ring is not. She is the adventure's moral fork: the players can have Lenz and leave her standing, or reach for her and learn what wealth does to evidence.

First Warden Aldous Crane. The guild's head, and a man carrying the quietest guilt in the story: he suspected the seal fraud and said nothing, because Vexley's money was saving the pension fund. He will test the party — with access, with skepticism, with small cruelties — to learn whether they are heading toward Lenz or toward him. If they show him mercy's shape, he will open the guild's records himself. He is your lever for the trial's ending.

Constable-Sergeant Ada Quill, of the city watch. Competent, overworked, and constitutionally unable to let amateurs out-investigate her — which means she will be the party's rival, their obstacle, and, if handled well, their eventual ally. She wants the case closed correctly, in that order of emphasis. Give her one scene per session; she is the pressure that keeps the clock running.

Willem, the apprentice. The obvious suspect and the innocent one: nineteen, gifted, and quietly stealing silver from the workshop to pay his sister's hospital fees. His secret is a red-herring with a heartbeat — when it surfaces, and it will, let it cost him something real, and let the party's treatment of him say who they are. Willem loved Thorne. He is also the only person who can identify the automaton's altered gait, because he oiled it weekly.

Marta Thorne, the widow. She knows her husband was afraid, and she knows of whom, and she will not say it to strangers until she has watched them for a scene or two. She holds the key to Thorne's hidden drawer — and the drawer is empty, because Lenz took the ledger, and her discovery of that emptiness is the moment the party understands the killer was a friend with a key.

Scrivener, the automaton. Not a character — a witness, a weapon, and a locked room, all in one brass body. It sits in the workshop where it was left, pen in hand, and it will do exactly what its drum tells it to do, forever, until someone unpins it. The scene in which the party unpins the drum and watches the night of the murder replay in brass — the bolt drawn, the clocks stopped, the key returned — is your set piece. Stage it in silence except for the ticking.

Chapter SixThe City & Its Places

The city is your board; these are its squares. The Guildhouse: the feast hall where the toast was given, the workshop where Thorne died (a sealed crime scene the watch guards badly), and the bell tower whose great clock, alone in the district, kept true time all night — a fact the players should be allowed to verify, because it corroborates the pocket watch. Thorne's house, above the workshop: Marta's domain, the hidden drawer, and the window that looks onto the canal. The canal district: Mrs. Anselm's shop, where gears are cut by a blind woman who hears them like music, and where the party will learn that the moth-marked gear is a commission from Vexley's household. The Meridian coffee house: where the guild's journeymen gossip, where Lenz holds court on Thursdays, and where a well-bought round of coffee is worth three rumor rolls. The watch-house: Quill's fortress, where the party will be alternately welcomed, stonewalled, and, if they are careless, investigated themselves. And the automaton quarter, across the river: where Scrivener's kind are built and repaired, and where a party that brings the brass beetle key will find a repairman who remembers Lenz asking, three weeks before the murder, how a program drum is re-pinned — "for a friend," Lenz said, and the repairman laughed at the time, and will not laugh now.

Run the city as a rumor engine. Every location should hand the party one fact and one flavor, and no location should ever be the only place a fact can be found. If the players skip the canal district, Mrs. Anselm takes her tea at the Meridian on Fridays. If they never visit the watch-house, Quill comes to the funeral, because it is her job to watch who cries. The clue web in Chapter Four is a net, not a path; your city is how you keep the net moving.

Chapter SevenThe Shape of the Sessions

Session One: the body and the room. Open at the funeral — rain, guild banners, a eulogy Lenz delivers beautifully — and then the party is drawn in: by Marta, by Quill, by the guild, or by their own noses. The workshop scene is your first hour: let them find C1 (the gear), C2 (the stopped clocks), C3 (the pocket watch), and the sealed room itself, in any order, with generous rolls and failure-forward costs. Introduce Quill as the obstacle with a badge, and Lenz as the grief that demands solving. End the session with a hook that pulls: the automaton's fresh oil (C5), or Marta's line about the friend, or — if the table is quick — the first crack in Lenz's alibi. If the watch arrests Willem on circumstantial evidence, so much the better; a wrong arrest gives session two its moral engine.

Session Two: the web. This is the investigation session, and your job is to keep six balls in the air: the gear's provenance (C1→C6), the true time (C3+C4+C8), the locked room's method (C5+C7+C8), the motive (C10+C11), Vexley's ring (C12), and Lenz's growing awareness that he is being hunted. Let the party split, if they wish — this cast supports two simultaneous scenes — and let the city hand out clues per Chapter Six. Midway through, spring the set piece: Lenz moves. A fire at the watch-house evidence room, small and surgical, aimed at Scrivener — the automaton survives if the party thought to move it, and is damaged if they did not, and either way the attempt tells them the killer knows the machine is the witness. Close the session with the repairman's testimony or the chimney sweep's light, so the table goes into session three holding an indictment they have built with their own hands.

Session Three: the reckoning. The party has the shape of the truth; now they choose its use. Three paths, all valid. The guild court: an accusation before Crane and the wardens, where the party must present a chain of evidence — run it as a structured scene, clue by clue, with Lenz's advocate poking holes in any link the table cannot support. The trap: a forged page of the lost ledger, "recovered," and a room with the door left open — Lenz will come for it at night, and Quill will be waiting, if the party has earned her. The deal: Vexley's offer — Lenz for the ring's exposure, a trade of one justice for another, and a moral fork the table will argue about for weeks. Whichever path they choose, the confession scene is the same, because Lenz has been waiting for it. Then the verdict, and its price, and the last scene at Thorne's grave, where Marta says the only line that matters: "He would have wanted you to be sure. He was always sure."

Chapter EightRed Herrings & Dead Ends

Four herrings, each a real story that is not this story. Willem's silver: the apprentice is stealing, and his sister is sick, and when the theft surfaces the watch will want to close the case on him — the party must spend a scene or a favor to keep an innocent boy out of the river. Marta's suitor: a river merchant, kind and persistent, whom one player will be certain is the killer; he is not, and his only secret is that he has asked Marta twice and been refused gently. The rival guild's offer: the Instrumentmakers have been courting Thorne's workshop for years, and their agent's suspicious timing is coincidence with money behind it. And the winding key: presented early as a herring — "Lenz carries a stopped watch, how sentimental" — and revealed late as C7, a real clue wearing a disguise. That last one is your craft lesson in miniature: the best red herring is a true clue the table has not yet earned.

Chapter NineThe Reveal & The Trial

Adjudicating the accusation. Whether the party goes to the guild court, the trap, or the watch, run the proof as a checklist against the chains in Chapter Four: the method (how the room locked), the time (why 11:47 lies), the means (the gear, the needle, the drum), and the motive (the manifests). Each chain the party can present intact removes one of Lenz's defenses; present all four and his advocate sits down. Present two, and the case is circumstantial — Crane will still act, because Crane has his own reasons, but the ending is messier, and messier is fine.

The confession. If the table earns it, read this aloud:

Read Aloud

"'I have rehearsed this,' Lenz says, 'so I will say it plainly. Elias was going to ruin me, and he was going to do it kindly, which I could not bear. I went to beg. I left a key's turn too late, and the begging had become something else. The room I locked, because I am a clockmaker, and I could not leave the evening unwound. The clocks I stopped, because I needed the hour I was on the podium. The gear he took from the machine — I did not know he had it. I have thought, every night since, that if he had only taken my hand instead, I might have been able to stop.' He looks at his own hands, a long moment. 'He took the gear. You see what I mean. He was always sure.'"

The verdict. The guild can hang Lenz, or transport him, or — if the party argues mercy and the table has earned Crane — let him live with the knowledge, which is its own transport. Vexley, unless the party reaches for her, will watch the whole proceeding from a carriage, and nod, once, to whoever meets her eye. Aftermath: the guild's favor, Scrivener unpinned and free, and seeds — Vexley's patron above her, the ledger still missing, and a moth-marked gear that Mrs. Anselm will tell you, if you ask, she cut eleven of.

Chapter TenRewards & Seeds

Rewards. The guild pays for the solving — coin, and more usefully, the guild's seal on any future door. Scrivener, unpinned, will serve the party that freed it: it cannot speak, but it can write, carry, remember, and hold a door against four men. Marta will give the party Elias's traveling kit, and inside it, folded, the first page of the missing ledger — he had split it, the old fox, and the hunt for the rest is a campaign, not an adventure.

Seeds. Lady Vexley's patron, above her, has never been named. The missing ledger is in the wind, and three parties want it. Mrs. Anselm cut eleven moth gears; ten are accounted for. And in the automaton quarter, a repairman has begun asking questions of his own, because the man who asked about program drums paid in guild script — and the serial on that script was Crane's.

Chapter ElevenRunning It

Your notes. Keep a clue tracker: the numbers C1–C12, with a mark for found, missed, and cost-paid. Review it between sessions. The web is resilient, but you cannot improvise its resilience unless you can see it. Keep a cast sheet too: where Lenz is at all times, what Quill knows, what Vexley has offered. The players will surprise you; the tracker is how you surprise them back, fairly.

When they skip a node. They will. The cure is never to drag them back; it is to let the net move — Mrs. Anselm at the Meridian, Quill at the funeral, the repairman's testimony arriving as a letter. Every clue has legs; use them.

Keeping the killer human. The temptation, around session two, is to sharpen Lenz into a villain — the sneer, the slip, the monologue. Resist it. Lenz is a man who is grieving the man he killed, and the players will feel the difference between a murderer and a tragedy, and the tragedy is the better game. His tells are small: the stopped watch, the unwound clocks, the rehearsal. Trust the small tells.

Tone. Rain on the canals, gaslight on wet stone, and everywhere, ticking — the district's clocks, the workshop's silence, the bell tower's true time. Let sound do the atmosphere. Read the aloud passages at half your normal pace.

Adaptation. The method travels: the automaton can be a drone, a familiar, a house-spirit, a smart-home system — the locked room is a machine that follows instructions, in any century. For a longer campaign, the missing ledger and the eleven gears are your spine. For a one-shot, open at the workshop, pre-seed C1–C4 as found, and run sessions two and three as one long evening.

Last counsel. You are running the only genre where the players' cleverness is the product. Your victory condition is the table shouting the solution before you have finished reading the confession. Everything in this tome is in service of that shout. Protect it.

Chapter OneWhat This Tome Is

This tome is a complete pulp expedition adventure for three to six players, written for the game master, and it runs three to five sessions depending on how long your table lingers on the river. It is a story with the engine of a serial: a lost road, a sunken city, a rival expedition, a star that fell, and a clock made of weather and ambition. It is system-agnostic; the suggested rolls and stat sketches translate to any system where competent people do dangerous things.

The pitch in one breath: four hundred years ago, the city of Xara Veth closed its gates and the jungle took the road that led to it. The road still exists — the Serpent Road, switchbacking up a river no map names — but it can only be walked with a key: the jade compass, broken in two halves when the road's last warden fled. One half sits in the vault of the Brass Compass Company, a salvage outfit with investors and a deadline. The other hangs around the neck of Bastion, the road's last warden, in a debtor's quarter of the port city of Porto Sol, drinking himself out of ever going back. The players are the expedition that puts the halves together — or the reason the halves never meet.

The tone to aim for is the Saturday serial: the danger is real, people die, the river does not care — but courage is rewarded, style counts double, and every session should end on a cliff the players can see from a boat. Play the rivals as mirrors, not villains. Play the jungle as a participant, not a backdrop. And never let a session end in a safe harbor twice in a row.

Chapter TwoThe Truth

Warden's Margin

What actually waits at the end of the road. The players will learn it in pieces; you must hold the whole.

Xara Veth did not fall; it chose. Four centuries ago, the city's astronomers found a star that had fallen into the high valley — a shard of sky-metal that hummed, and healed, and whispered in mathematics. The city split over it: the Gate faction would have sold the star's secrets to the lowland empires; the Canopy faction would have kept them. The Canopy won, closed the gates, and let the jungle write the city off the map. The star — the Sunken Star, it is called now, because the city sank half of itself into a cenote to hide the star's light — is a beacon. Dormant, patient, and pointed at something very far away. It has been dormant for four hundred years because the Canopy's descendants still keep it: a remnant of the city lives in the canopy above the ruins, counting the years, waiting for the day someone brings the road back to their door.

The Brass Compass Company knows part of this. Their investors want the star-metal for armories — sky-metal that hums is sky-metal that can be made to scream — and their director, Kessler, is a professional who will get it if the road allows. What no one knows, including Kessler, is the last line of the Canopy's count: the star wakes when it is moved, and when it wakes, it calls. The adventure's final choice is the star's fate — leave it, sell it, wake it, or sink it — and every faction on the river will be at the table when the players decide.

One more truth, for your ears only: the previous expedition — the Company's, twelve years ago — reached the gate of roots. One woman walked out. Her name is in Kessler's files, her journal is in his lockbox, and Bastion's daughter wrote every page of it.

Chapter ThreeHooks & Session Zero

Hooks. The Antiquarian Society of Porto Sol will fund an expedition to trace the Serpent Road — their money is thin and their faith is real, and they will give the party something the Company cannot: legitimacy. The personal hook is Bastion himself: every party needs the warden, and the warden needs a reason, and the reason you will use is his daughter — lost on the Company's expedition twelve years ago, her fate unknown, her journal in Kessler's lockbox. Tell the players, at session zero, that someone in the party may be connected to the previous expedition: a parent who never came home, a sponsor who went bankrupt, a name on a memorial wall. Let them build it; you will use all of it. The rival hook is the auction: the Company is selling the previous expedition's effects — including, lot 47, a journal the Company claims is "water-damaged, unsalvageable" and will in fact burn rather than surrender.

Session zero. Set the serial's contract: this is pulp — characters are competent, death is possible but never random, and every failure should be interesting enough to applaud. Establish the party's roles — guide, scholar, doctor, muscle, scoundrel, patron's agent — and one shared debt, because expeditions are held together by money and grudges. Run your safety tools around the jungle's horrors: the fever, the drownings, and the canopy people's justice are the edges. And agree on the tone: the river is the clock, the Company is the mirror, and the city is the question.

Chapter FourThe Cast

Bastion, the last warden. Sixty, built like a river piling, with a jade compass half around his neck and a debt-collector's notice on his door. Bastion walked the Serpent Road for twenty years and came out the last time carrying his daughter's empty saddle, and he has not been sober or honest about either since. He will refuse the party twice — once for pride, once for fear — and relents when they offer him the one price he cannot refuse: a grave for his daughter, if they find one, in the city she died reaching. Play him as a man whose competence is intact and whose reasons are ruined; on the river, he is the best guide alive, and he knows it, and it does not help him. Stat sketch: master of the river, hard to kill, will not leave a body behind, drinks when the party sleeps.

Director-Sergeant Major Kessler, the Brass Compass Company. A professional of the old school: precise, courteous, and utterly convinced that the star belongs to whoever can carry it. Kessler is not cruel; he is worse — he is reasonable, and his reasonableness has a payroll. He respects competence above everything, which means the party can earn his parley and, in the third act, his alliance — but only if they have beaten him fairly at least once. He keeps Bastion's daughter's journal in his lockbox, not as leverage but as a debt he has never worked out how to pay. Play him as the mirror: everything the party is, with a company behind it. Stat sketch: veteran officer, disciplined guard, two lieutenants, a machinegun the river will eventually claim.

Dr. Livia Moreau, the Company's savant. A genuine astronomer in a salvage outfit, and the only person in the Company who knows what the star actually is — not metal, but a message. She is conflicted, recruitable, and the adventure's hinge: if the party turns her, the third act changes shape; if they do not, she will stand in the star chamber and argue for the sky, and she will be right, and it will be terrible. Play her as the smartest person in every room who has learned to say it quietly.

Sable, "Mother of Eels," captain of the River Saints. Pirate-priestess of the river's middle reaches: she takes tolls, she baptizes boats, she collects stories instead of gold, because stories are the only cargo that gets heavier when you spend it. Sable is the river's face — capricious, generous, exact — and her toll scene is the adventure's best roleplay set piece. She hated the Company on sight twelve years ago, and she will love or hate the party based entirely on the story they tell her.

Ixchel-Who-Counts, speaker of the canopy remnant. The last of the Canopy faction's line, keeper of the count, and the person the star is, in a sense, waiting for. Ixchel speaks in numbers — "you are the fourth party; the third came with iron; you bring a woman who knows the sky" — and their test of the party is a test of attention: they must count what the city has lost, which means they must learn the city, which means they must listen. Play Ixchel as neither hostile nor welcoming: they are an accountant of four hundred years, and the party is an entry.

Tomas, the expedition's cook and muleteer. Tomas is nobody's hero and everybody's heart: he feeds the party, names the mules, and tells the story of the road's last warden better than Bastion can, because he heard it from Bastion's daughter, years ago, in a kitchen in Porto Sol. In Act Two, the fever will take Tomas, and the party's choice — press on or lose three days — is the adventure's moral hinge. Keep him alive if you can. The party will love him more if they almost did not.

Chapter FiveThe Road

The Serpent Road is your board: twelve hexes upriver, each a day's travel under oar and pole, each with a face and a hazard. Run it as a track, not a map — the party's position is a number from one to twelve, and the river is the rail. The hexes, in order: Porto Sol's harbor; the lower river, wide and patrolled; the falls, where the road portages around white water; the fever flats, where the river slows and the air thickens; the broken bridge, where the old road's span hangs by three ropes over a gorge that has learned patience; the whispering grove, where the jungle's noise stops for a quarter mile; the River Saints' toll island; the Company's forward camp, a scar of canvas and lamplight; the narrows, where the river turns to a green corridor; the gate of roots, where the jade halves must work together; the climb, a switchback stair grown over; and Xara Veth itself, half-sunk in its cenote, roots like columns, canopy like a second sky.

Four tracks run the road. Supplies: the party starts with ten days; every hex eats one, every delay eats more, and foraging is a roll with teeth — the jungle will feed you and then invoice you. Morale: the party and its crew start steady; the fever, the drownings, and the night drums will test it, and at zero the expedition does not end — it renegotiates, which is worse. The river level: the dry season is ending, and every week the river drops a hand's width, which means the narrows will close behind them — the clock is made of water. And the Company's lead: Kessler is three hexes ahead at the start, and his distance is a number you move every time the party hesitates and every time they act.

Weather die. Each morning, roll. Rain means the portage doubles, the bridge slicks, the fever flats steam. Wind means the sails work or the tents go. Sun means the river turns to a mirror and the party sees, for one hex, the road as it was — four hundred years of jungle, and under it, a straight line, waiting. That last result is free atmosphere; use it once per session at most, and let it ache.

Chapter SixAct One — The Auction & The Assembly

Act One is Porto Sol, and it is the serial's opening reel: color, noise, a fistfight, and a departure on the evening tide. Structure it in four beats.

The auction. The Company is selling the previous expedition's effects in a warehouse on the harbor — water-stained tents, a theodolite with a cracked lens, and lot 47, a journal, "unsalvageable." The auction is where the party learns three things: the jade compass exists (Bastion bids on lot 12, a compass case, with money he does not have), the Company is afraid of the journal (their agent bids against himself to keep it from the room), and Kessler is watching the room with the patience of a man who has done this before. Let the party acquire the journal however their scoundrel prefers — the bid, the lift, the swap — and let them read one page aloud at the session's end: Bastion's daughter's hand, describing the gate of roots, and the last line: "The road remembers who walks it. Tell my father the road was never lost. We were."

Bastion. The party finds him in the debtor's quarter, and the scene is a negotiation with a man who has already lost: he will refuse, he will drink, and he will name his price — the grave, in the city, for the daughter — because the party has the journal, and the journal is proof she reached the gate. He relents at dawn, sober, with his half of the compass around his neck and nothing else.

The assembly. A montage with teeth: hiring the crew, buying the mules, chartering the riverboat — the Mariblanca, a stern-wheeler with opinions — and the Company's counter-moves, each one a small scene: Kessler buying out the pilots, an offer to the party's best hand to switch sides, a quiet fire in the Mariblanca's hold that the crew puts down in time, barely. Every counter-move the party defeats earns them a hex of lead; every one they miss costs the same.

The fistfight. The serial needs its first set piece, and the dock at dusk is where it happens: the Company's stevedores, the party's crew, a crate that goes overboard, and Bastion, who has not thrown a punch in twelve years, ending the fight by throwing one man into the river and apologizing to the river. Read the departure aloud:

Read Aloud

"Porto Sol lets you go the way it lets everything go — loudly, and with advice. The harbor lights swing past, the pilot jumps for the last boat, and the Mariblanca's wheel takes the current with a shudder you feel in your teeth. Astern, the city is a rumor of lamps. Ahead, the river is black and certain, and somewhere up it, twelve hexes and four hundred years, a road is waiting to remember what it is. Bastion, at the bow, does not look back. 'First rule of the road,' he says, to no one, to everyone. 'The river decides. You only vote.'"

End the act on the water, with the Company's smoke on the horizon and the river's clock started.

Chapter SevenAct Two — The River & The Road

Act Two is the road itself, and it is the heart of the tome: the hex track in motion, the set pieces strung along it, and the slow conversion of the Company from rival to fact of life. Run it as a sequence of choices with the four tracks ticking — supplies, morale, river level, Company lead — and let the set pieces arise from the hexes.

The falls portage. Hex three: the Mariblanca cannot climb the white water, and everything — boat pieces, mules, crates, people — must go overland on a path the jungle has been reclaiming for four centuries. Run it as a skill scene: every player contributes, every roll moves a crate or costs an hour, and at the top, the river reassembles itself below them, and one mule is gone, and the jungle has issued its first invoice. This scene's job is to teach the table the expedition's grammar: effort, cost, momentum.

The fever flats. Hex four, and the river slows to a green mirror, and the air becomes something you chew. Tomas goes down on the second day — the fever, the real one, not the romantic kind. Here is the hinge: press on and Tomas rides in the shade of a sail, tended by whoever has the gentlest hands, and the party loses nothing but sleep; or camp three days at the flats' edge, burn supplies, and let the Company's lead widen to a number that will matter at the gate. Either choice is correct; neither is free. Play Tomas's fever straight — he will talk, in it, about Bastion's daughter, and what she said in the kitchen, and the party will lean in, because sick men tell the truth.

The broken bridge. Hex five: the old road's span, three ropes and rotten planks over a gorge, and on the far side, the Company's sabotage team — four men with knives and orders, who cut the first rope before the parley starts. Run the crossing as a set piece: the party on the bridge, the ropes going one by one, the gorge below patient, and Kessler's lieutenant on the far bank discovering, in real time, that his orders were worse than his men. The bridge will take someone's blood or someone's pride; let the table choose which.

The toll island. Hex seven, and the River Saints' ground: Sable's island, a chapel of boat-ribs and candles, and the toll. Sable does not want gold. She wants the party's best story — the true one, the one that costs — and she will know if the telling is cheap. This is the adventure's quiet set piece, and often its best: a circle of lanterns, a pirate-priestess with an eel around her shoulders, and a player discovering what their character actually carries. Her blessing is worth two hexes of favor; her curse is worth remembering. She will also tell them, if the story is good enough, what the Company did twelve years ago at the gate — "they knocked," she will say, "and the road answered" — and what the knocking cost.

The forward camp. Hex eight: Kessler's canvas city, lamplit against the dark, and the first real parley. Kessler will offer an alliance at the gate — his half of the compass for the party's, a joint walk, a divided star — and the offer is honest, because Kessler is honest about arithmetic: the gate needs both halves, and neither expedition has both. The party can take the deal, refuse it, or steal his half, and Dr. Moreau, at his elbow, will look at whichever player meets her eye. And in his lockbox, visible for one moment, the journal's twin — Bastion's daughter's field notes, the ones the auction journal was copied from. Bastion will see them. Plan for what he does.

The gate of roots. Hex ten, and the road's threshold: a wall of living roots across the valley, and in its center, a socket shaped like a compass, whole. The halves must work together — alliance, theft, or a trade the table invents — and when the jade turns, the roots open like a hand, and the road, four hundred years patient, begins to climb. Read the opening aloud, and end the session on the first switchback, with the canopy dark above and, very faintly, the sound of counting.

Chapter EightAct Three — Xara Veth

Act Three is the city, and it is the serial's last reel: wonder first, then the reckoning. The climb takes a session's first hour — the switchback stair, the canopy closing overhead — and the first sight of Xara Veth, which you should stage as a reveal, not an arrival. Read this aloud when the party breaches the canopy's edge:

Read Aloud

"The city is half-drowned in its own hiding. Below you, the cenote opens like a mouth the jungle has been holding shut for four hundred years — and in it, Xara Veth, sunk to its shoulders, towers going down into green dark, bridges between them gone to vines, and everywhere the roots, thick as temple columns, holding the city up and holding it down at once. Above the ruin, in the canopy, lights move. Not firelight. Something steadier. And from everywhere and nowhere, a sound like an abacus the size of a cathedral: the count, still running, four hundred years of it, and it has noticed you."

The canopy remnant. Ixchel-Who-Counts receives the party on a bridge of living wood, and their test is attention: the party must count what the city has lost — its people, its gates, its years — which means walking the ruin, listening, and learning the story the stones keep. Run it as an exploration scene with the count as its reward: every truth the party learns about the city is a number Ixchel confirms, and every confirmed number brings the star chamber closer. The star, they will learn, is not a treasure; it is a guest, and it has been waiting, and the Canopy's count is not a vigil — it is a lullaby.

The star chamber. Beneath the cenote's waterline, in a hall the roots have held open: the Sunken Star, a shard of sky the size of a man's torso, humming in a mathematics the party feels in their teeth. And waiting — by alliance, by theft, by force, depending on the road behind them — Kessler, with Moreau at his shoulder and the Company's last strength at his back. The choice resolves here, and every faction is at the table: leave the star, and the city sleeps another four hundred years, and the Company goes home empty and Bastion gets his grave; give it to Kessler, and the party is rich and the sky is someone else's problem, and Moreau will tell them, quietly, exactly what the armories will learn; wake it, and the beacon calls, and the adventure ends on a cliff the whole campaign will spend itself climbing down; or sink it — Bastion's choice, if the party lets him have one — the star down into the cenote's throat, the city sinking with it, gently, at last, and the escape a set piece of roots and water and counting that stops, mid-number, and starts again, slower, at peace.

The escape, if they choose it, is the serial's last reel: the cenote going down, the canopy people climbing, Kessler's machinegun singing one useless verse, and the Mariblanca — if she still floats, if the river still allows — taking them out under a sky that has begun, very faintly, to answer. End the act on the river, downstream, with the city gone from the map again, and let each player say one thing their character keeps from the road. That is the treasure that survives the serial.

Chapter TenRewards & Aftermath

Treasure. Star-metal, if the table allows it: a shard the size of a fist, humming, worth a fortune and a subpoena. Jade from the gate, which the Canopy will let the party keep, because jade remembers the road and will come home eventually. Bastion's daughter's field notes, which the Antiquarian Society will publish over the Company's objections, and which will make the party's names — or their aliases. And the Mariblanca, if she survives, which is its own reward, and her captain will tell you so.

Aftermath. Bastion gets his grave, or he does not, and either scene is worth playing in full. Kessler, if he survives, files his report honestly: "Expedition successful. Asset declined acquisition." Moreau, if turned, publishes the star's mathematics under her own name, and the sky begins, very slowly, to be a problem. Sable collects the story of the road from every port on the river, and it grows in the telling, as she intended.

Seeds. The beacon, if woken, has been answered — the campaign's next volume writes itself. The Company's investors do not accept "declined acquisition," and the next expedition will not be led by a man who apologizes to rivers. And the count, in the canopy, has resumed — but it is counting something new now, and Ixchel, if the party returns, will tell them the number, and let them decide what it means.

Chapter ElevenRunning It

Momentum is the engine. The serial dies when the boat stops. Keep at least two clocks visible at all times — the river level and the Company's lead — and whenever the table hesitates, move one. Never punish hesitation with combat; punish it with distance. Kessler three hexes ahead is a rumor; Kessler at the gate first is a fact.

Set pieces, not encounters. Every hex should offer either a set piece (the bridge, the toll, the portage) or atmosphere, and never two of the same kind in a row. A set piece is an encounter with an audience and an exit; run each one with a clear escalation, a clear cost, and a clear button — the moment the scene ends, on a line, not a whimper.

Pulp failure. In this tome, failure is never "nothing happens." Failure is the crate going overboard, the rope cutting early, the story told cheap — interesting, costly, and forward. If a roll fails and the table groans with delight, you have the genre right.

The rivals. Kessler is the mirror: beat him fairly once, and he will parley; beat him twice, and he will ally; humiliate him, and the third act becomes a war. Moreau is the hinge: she is watching the party the whole river, deciding. Let her decide in play, not in your notes.

Adaptation. For a tighter three sessions, compress Act One to the auction and the departure, and let the falls and the flats share a session. For a five-session epic, give the toll island and the forward camp each a full evening. For a one-shot, open at the gate of roots with both halves in play, and run the city as a single long session ending on the star's choice.

Last counsel. This tome is about the road as a test of character: what the party carries up the river is what they bring to the star. Keep the water moving, keep the mirror close, and when the city opens like a hand, let it be quiet. The serial's last trick is that the wonder was never the treasure. It was the counting.

Chapter OneWhat This Tome Is

This tome is a complete colony-management campaign for three to six players, written for the game master, and it runs six to ten sessions across four in-game seasons. It is different from the other tomes in this folio, and it should be: the players are not wandering a dungeon or chasing a signal. They are the council of a settlement — Ashfall Reach, a new colony on a frontier coast, under a volcano that has begun, very politely, to clear its throat. The adventure is the colony itself: its food, its walls, its quarrels, its charter, and the mountain that underwrites everything.

The genre's promise, and your job: management is not bookkeeping, it is drama with arithmetic in it. Every resource is a person. Every shortage is a scene. Every surplus is a claimant. The colony's ledger is not a sidebar to the story — it is the story's spine, and the seasons are its acts. If your table has never played a management campaign, tell them this: you will spend sessions arguing about grain, and they will be some of the best sessions of their lives, because the grain will have a name, and the name will have a family, and the family will be at the council door when the tally comes up short.

The tome gives you the setting and its ledger, the players' roles, the cast, the season turn that structures every session, four seasons of campaign arc — landing, roots, quickening, ashwinter — and the event tables that keep the colony from ever being a spreadsheet. It is system-agnostic; the tracks are five numbers from zero to ten, and any dice your table already owns will do the rest.

The tone to aim for is ledger and hearth: the colony is a small warm place in a large cold world, and every decision is a choice about who stays warm. The Company is not evil; it is distant. The cult is not mad; it is attentive. The mountain is not a villain; it is a fact. Your job is to make the facts argue.

Chapter TwoThe Setting & The Ledger

Ashfall Reach sits at the mouth of the River Cinder, on a coast the maps call the Burnt Shoulder, under Mount Cinder — a volcano that erupted once, three hundred years ago, and has been considered finished ever since, by everyone except the people who live on it. The colony is two years old at the campaign's start: a palisade, a dock, a granary, forty-one souls, and a charter from the Meridian Company, which owns the land, the export rights, and, in the fine print, "all that lies beneath." The ash comes in seasons: light dustings in spring, heavier falls in autumn, and the old people — the River Folk upstream — say the pattern is changing. The River Folk are right.

The ledger is your engine. Five tracks, each zero to ten, each with a face. Food — Old Gran Meera's tally stick, kept in the granary door; at zero, the colony eats its seed grain, and then it eats its pride. Shelter — the wall, the roofs, Captain Holt's nightly rounds; at zero, the winter gets inside. Morale — the colony's temper, kept by no one and felt by everyone; at zero, the boats start leaving at night. Defense — the palisade, the watch, the two crossbows the Company calls an armory; at zero, the river's wolves and the coast's raiders do the arithmetic for you. And the Charter — the colony's standing with the Company, its paper soul; at zero, the ships stop coming, and the colony is free, which is another word for alone.

The tracks move on the season turn, detailed in Chapter Five, but the principle is simple: every season, the colony consumes (Food drops by the number of mouths, modified by the harvest), every project the council builds moves a track up, and every event moves something down — or up, because the mountain gives as well as takes. At each season's end, any track at zero produces a crisis, and crises are scenes, not numbers: a granary riot, a wall breach, a midnight boat, a letter from the Company that begins with the word "regretfully."

The factions. The Meridian Company: distant, ledger-minded, not evil — its factor, Penhale, has quotas, and quotas are a kind of weather. The Freeholders: the settlers who crossed the sea for land and are discovering the charter's fine print, led by Gran Meera, who remembers what the last colony's fine print did. The Ash-Cult — the colony calls them the Gray Brethren — settlers who have noticed the mountain's pattern and drawn the only conclusion the evidence supports, led by a former Company engineer called the Gray Prophet, who saw Cinder wake on a survey map and has not been right about anything else since, which is the tragedy of him. And the River Folk: upstream, old, patient, trading bark and river-craft, led by Selka-of-Two-Boats, who will tell you the mountain's history if you will stop calling it finished.

Chapter ThreeThe Players' Roles

The players are the colony's council, and each holds an office that answers for a track. The Warden answers for Defense — the wall, the watch, the river's threats. The Steward answers for Food — the granary, the fields, the fish, the hunt. The Voice answers for Morale — justice, feasts, funerals, the colony's temper. The Factor answers for the Charter — trade, quotas, the Company's letters, Penhale's visits. The Surveyor answers for Shelter — the wall's stones, the roofs, the leat, every project with a cost and a season. With three players, merge Voice into Factor and Shelter into Warden; with six, add the Clerk, who keeps the charter's copy and answers for nothing except the truth, which in this campaign is a track of its own.

The council is the session's spine: each season, every officer proposes a project or a policy, the ledger shows what the colony can afford, and the vote — at your table, with real stakes, real arguments, and real consequences — decides. Give each office a domain the others cannot command: the Warden cannot order the granary opened, but can stand at its door. The friction is the game. And give each player, at session zero, one private charge from their faction — Gran Meera's Freeholders will whisper to the Steward; the Brethren will find the Voice — because a council of pure common interest is a spreadsheet, and a council of competing loyalties is a colony.

You, the game master, play the colony as an NPC: its moods, its rumors, its doors. When the ledger moves, someone knocks. That is the whole craft, and it is enough.

Chapter FourThe Cast

Aldous Penhale, the Company's factor. A ledger-man with a ledger-man's virtues: he is exact, he is fair within his instructions, and his instructions are the problem. Penhale wants the colony to meet its quota — salted fish, timber, pitch — because the Company's patience is measured in shipments, and because his own pension is measured in the colony's survival, a fact he will never say aloud. He is your lever for the Company's face: reasonable in person, ruthless on paper, and genuinely fond of the colony in the way a man is fond of a garden he has been ordered to prune. Play him as an ally the players cannot fully trust and an obstacle they cannot fully hate.

Old Gran Meera, heart of the Freeholders. Seventy, sharp as a fish-hook, keeper of the tally stick, and the colony's institutional memory — she crossed on the first ship, buried her husband on the second winter, and has been counting the Company's promises against its performances ever since. Meera wants title deeds: land the settlers own, not lease, and she will use every shortage, every season, and every favor to get them. She is the colony's conscience and its obstruction, often in the same sentence, and she is right often enough that the players will learn to dread her being right.

The Gray Prophet, voice of the Brethren. His name was Edran Kell, a Company survey engineer who mapped Cinder's vents twelve years ago and filed a report the Company sealed, because the report said the mountain was not finished and the lease said it was. Kell broke over the sealed report — not loudly, quietly, the way engineers break — and now he keeps the Brethren, who watch the ash, count the tremors, and prepare. He is not mad; he is the only person in the colony with data, and the data has made him terrible. Play him as precise, patient, and incapable of comfort: he will be right about the mountain, and wrong about everything the mountain makes people do, and he will not see the difference until the end.

Selka-of-Two-Boats, the River Folk's trader. Middle-aged, unhurried, fluent in three languages and the fourth one that is just silence. Selka trades river-bark, fever-cure, and the mountain's true history — which the River Folk keep in a song-cycle four nights long — to anyone who will pay attention. She is wary of the colony, because the last colony upstream is a place-name now, and her price for real help is an old wrong: the Company's first survey drove stakes through the River Folk's winter grounds, and the charter that the players administer is the document that did it. The colony's reckoning with that wrong is one of your campaign's moral tracks.

Captain Bran Holt, keeper of the wall. A soldier of the Company's colonial militia who chose the colony over the garrison, because the garrison had a cemetery and the colony had a future. Holt is the Defense track made flesh: he counts crossbow bolts the way Meera counts grain, and he will tell the council, every season, exactly how long the wall holds, in a voice that makes the number feel like a verdict. He respects the Brethren's watchfulness and fears their certainty, and his slow conversion — from company man to colony man — is worth a scene a season.

Dr. Iles, the colony's physician and its second scientist. Iles believes in bark, boiling water, and the mountain's chemistry, and is locked in a quiet war with the Prophet over the colony's mind — science and prophecy, both pointing at the same ash, drawing opposite comfort. Iles needs the River Folk's fever-bark, which the river only gives through Selka, which makes the physician the colony's most motivated diplomat. Play Iles as the person who says "we have time" and means it as a prescription, not a fact.

Chapter FiveThe Season Turn

Every session — roughly one per season, with the great seasons taking two — runs the same four phases. The repetition is the point: the colony's rhythm is the campaign's rhythm, and the players will learn to hear the turn coming.

Phase One, the Ledger. Open each session with the numbers, aloud: the tally stick, the wall, the temper, the bolts, the charter. Consumption happens — Food drops by mouths minus harvest; Shelter drops by weather; the Company's letter moves the Charter. Do this in ninety seconds, in front of the players, and let the numbers be a weather report, not a math lesson. Then the faces arrive: Gran Meera at the granary door, Holt with his count, someone's brother with a complaint. Every number that moved gets a knock.

Phase Two, the Council. Each officer proposes: a project, a policy, an expedition. Projects are the colony's verbs — granary, wall, leat, mill, dock, watchtower, school, chapel — each with a cost in Food and Shelter and a season to build, and each with a claimant: the leat needs the River Folk's survey, the chapel needs the Brethren's hands or their boycott, the watchtower needs Holt's bolts or Meera's grain. The council argues, votes, and commits. Your job is to make every project a fork: nothing is simply good, everything costs something the colony loves. Keep the project list short — eight is plenty — and let the table's choices, over four seasons, build a colony that looks like their arguments.

Phase Three, the Event. Two events per season, drawn or rolled from the season's table in Chapter Ten. Events are the world's vote: the storm, the fever, the stranger, the tremor, the Company's ship, the saint's day. Some events are weather, some are people, and the best ones are both — the supply ship delayed is a Food event and a Charter event and a scene on the dock with Penhale holding a letter he does not want to read aloud.

Phase Four, the Face. Close the session by converting the season's arithmetic back into people: the riot or the feast, the wall inspection in the rain, the Brethren's procession at the ash-fall, the birth, the burial, the letter read at the fire. This phase is where the ledger becomes story, and it is the phase you must never skip for time. A season that ends on numbers ends on nothing.

The season's end: the tracks settle, the crises resolve or compound, and the next season's weather report arrives. Four turns. One colony. Every choice remembered.

Chapter SixSeason One — The Landing

Season One is the colony's first full year under the players' council, and its theme is roots: what holds, what grows, what the charter allows. The colony starts at Food 6, Shelter 5, Morale 7, Defense 4, Charter 6 — alive, hopeful, and one bad season from the edge.

Open with the landing's anniversary — a feast, because colonies need feasts the way walls need stones — and then the ledger's first lesson: the Company's quota letter arrives, and it is larger than last year's. Salted fish and timber and pitch, by the autumn ship, or the spring shipment "must be reconsidered." Penhale reads it to the council with the expression of a man handing over a diagnosis. The season's fork is set: meet the quota and bleed the colony's own stores, or refuse and spend Charter. There is no third option yet; finding the third option is what the next three seasons are for.

The season's events, in suggested order. The storm: an autumn gale takes the supply ship Perseverance on the bar — a Food crisis in the making, a scene on the dock in rain, and a salvage expedition up the coast that is the season's field trip: what the party recovers from the wreck (barrels, seed, a chest of the Company's sealed correspondence) will define the winter. The first heavy ash-fall: a day of gray light, a Morale test, and the Brethren's first public procession — Kell standing in the ash with his arms open, and the colony deciding, face by face, what to believe. The boundary dispute: the River Folk's fishing weirs upstream, and the colony's new nets downstream, and Selka arriving in her two boats to explain, with terrible patience, that the river was old when the charter was young. The fork: trade for the weir-rights (Food up, Charter down, the Company's agent notes the "unauthorized arrangement") or post the garrison at the river's bend (Defense up, the River Folk's silence down, and a wrong the campaign will remember).

Close the season with the first harvest — barely. The tally stick's number is the session's last scene: Gran Meera counting, aloud, in the granary door, and the colony listening, and the number landing one short of comfortable, and everyone doing the winter's arithmetic in their heads at once. Read the season's end aloud:

Read Aloud

"The ash settles on the first frost, and the colony wears its year like a new coat — patched, warmed through, not yet shaped to the shoulders. In the granary, the tally stick has one notch fewer than it should, and nobody says it, because everybody has said it. Up the river, the mountain wears its first snow, and under the snow, if you ask the Brethren, something is counting. The winter is coming. The ledger is open. Someone knocks."

Season One's job is to teach the turn: numbers, council, event, face. By the frost, the table should be arguing about grain as if it were gold. It is.

Chapter SevenSeason Two — The Roots

Season Two is winter, and its theme is what the cold reveals. The tracks carry the autumn's scars forward — Food one lower than comfortable, Morale wearing thin, the Charter's letter unanswered — and the Company answers in its own way: it sends an auditor.

Mr. Fallow arrives on the first hard frost, with a clerk, a ledger of his own, and instructions to "regularize" the colony's accounts. Fallow is Penhale's inverse: where Penhale bends the instructions around the colony, Fallow bends the colony around the instructions. He is not corrupt; he is worse — he is sincere, and his sincerity has the Company's seal. His audit is the season's pressure: every unauthorized arrangement, every spent favor, every barrel from the wreck comes up, and the Charter track becomes a battlefield fought with paper. The fork: give Fallow what he wants — the weir-rights revoked, the Brethren's procession "noted," the colony's books bled white — or defy him and spend Charter like water, betting that the spring ship brings someone more reasonable. It will not.

The fever. Midwinter, and Dr. Iles's worst arithmetic: the fever comes up the river in the trade goods, and the colony's shelter — warm, close, winter-sealed — is its engine. This is the season's crisis of Shelter and Food at once: the sick cannot work, the well cannot leave the sick, and the cure — river-bark, the real kind — grows only upstream, on the River Folk's ground, and the weir-rights are exactly the coin Selka will name. Run the bark expedition as the season's field piece: up the river in the cold, to the River Folk's winter camp, where the song-cycle is four nights long and the colony's wrong is the first verse. What the players offer — apology, amendment, debt — sets the campaign's moral track for good.

The midwinter ash-fall, and the Brethren's procession at its height: Kell in the gray light, and the colony's temper — the Morale track — forking on whether the procession is permitted or forbidden. Permit it, and the Brethren grow, and the Company's letter grows colder. Forbid it, and the Brethren go quiet, which is not the same as gone. And the Freeholders' petition: Gran Meera, at the council, with forty-one names on a page — title deeds, now, while the colony still has the strength to ask. The Charter fork, again, wearing a different face.

Close the season with the thaw and the landslide: the leat project — the colony's water-course, its next harvest's insurance — buried under a hillside the winter loosened, and the spring's first work a grave-digging that is not for people. The ledger's lesson: the mountain votes too. Season Two's job is to make the table feel the colony as a body — sick, audited, arguing, alive — and to plant the two seeds that Season Three will harvest: the Prophet's data, and the charter's fine print.

Chapter EightSeason Three — The Quickening

Season Three is the year the mountain stops being a rumor, and its theme is the cost of being right. Open with the signs, and do not be subtle: the river runs warm in the shallows, the tremors come twice a month and then twice a week, the ash-falls thicken, and the Brethren's count — which the colony has been mocking for two years — is suddenly the most accurate document in the settlement. Kell is right, and being right has made him unbearable, and the season's first scenes are the colony learning that a prophet is the least useful thing a true prophecy can be wrapped in.

The Company's truth. Fallow's audit has a sequel: the spring ship brings a survey team, and the survey team's maps, and the maps say what Kell's sealed report said twelve years ago — the colony sits on Cinder's vent field, and the charter's fine print, "all that lies beneath," was never about minerals. The Company knew. The colony was placed here because the vent field's ash makes the finest pitch in the hemisphere, and the Company's actuaries priced the eruption against the pitch and found the pitch winning. This is the season's fulcrum: the Charter track's meaning inverts. The Company is not the colony's patron; it is the colony's creditor, and the debt is the colony itself. The fork: publish the maps and break with the Company (Charter to zero, freedom, and the spring ships stop), or hold the knowledge and hold the charter, and begin quietly preparing for the day the mountain collects.

The River Folk's offer. Selka comes downstream with her two boats and, for the first time, a third: the River Folk will move the colony upstream, through the rapids, to the old ground above the flood — boats for everyone, bark for the fever, a place at the fire — at a price the song-cycle names: the charter burned, the Company's stakes pulled, the wrong amended in public, on the river, in the old way. The offer is genuine and the price is genuine, and the table will split over it, because it is the colony's soul on the ledger.

The Brethren's split. Kell's certainty, under pressure, fractures his own house: the moderates, who would prepare and stay, and the offering faction, who have begun to argue that the mountain wants something, and that what it wants is the charter's burning and the colony's humbling. The split is the Morale track's crisis, and it lands in the players' council as two petitions with the same first line and opposite last ones. And the harvest fails — the ash thickens through the summer, the fields gray, the tally stick's number the worst in the colony's short history. Gran Meera, in the granary door, does not count aloud this time. That silence is the season's last scene.

Close Season Three with the first real tremor — not the polite ones: the church bell rings by itself, the river jumps its banks a hand's width, and the mountain, for one evening, wears a plume against the sunset, and the colony watches it from every door. Read the plume aloud, and let the silence after it be the session's end. The quickening is over. The ashwinter is coming, and every fork the table has taken is about to be collected.

Chapter NineSeason Four — The Ashwinter

Season Four is the eruption arc, and it should be played as one long held breath that ends in the loudest scene of the campaign. Its theme is collection: every track, every fork, every favor and wrong, comes due.

The foreshocks. The season opens with the mountain's argument becoming daily: tremors, steam in the river shallows, ash-falls that close the sky by noon. The Brethren's count is now the colony's clock. The ledger becomes a countdown — Food for the road, Shelter for the choice, Defense for the panic, Morale for the night, Charter for the Company's last offer. Run the season's council as a war-council: the colony must choose its ashwinter, and the choices are the campaign's harvest.

The exodus. If the table took the River Folk's offer — or makes it now, at the higher price the war-council commands — the season is a logistics set piece: forty-one souls, two seasons of stores, the boats, the rapids, and the mountain's temper as the clock. Run it as a sequence of crises on the water — a boat swamped, a birth in the rapids, the Company's agent choosing, at the last, which side of the ledger he is on — and let every track the players kept above zero pay for itself here. Food 7 means the boats are fed. Defense 6 means the rapids are watched. Morale 7 means the song starts on its own.

The stand. If the table stays — for the pitch, for the land, for the stubborn arithmetic of home — the season is an engineering race: the leat, rebuilt and extended, as a mud-flow channel; the wall, doubled against the flood, not the raid; the granary, raised; the watchtower, given its true purpose at last. Kell's data and Iles's chemistry and Holt's bolts and Meera's grain, four seasons of arguments, become one machine for keeping forty-one people alive under a falling sky. The stand is the management genre's promise fulfilled: the spreadsheet becomes a fortress.

The Company's ship. Whatever the colony chooses, the Company makes its last offer on the ashwinter's eve: evacuation — ships, real ones, the Company's own — in exchange for the mineral rights, signed and sealed, "all that lies beneath" finally named. Penhale brings the offer himself, and for the first time his instructions and his conscience are on the same page, which makes him dangerous and pitiable at once. The fork: the bitter rescue, the colony saved and owned, or the refusal, and the ships going down the coast empty.

The eruption. Read it aloud, whatever else you do:

Read Aloud

"The mountain does not roar. That is the first thing, and the thing the survivors will keep correcting: it does not roar, it opens, like a door that has been stuck for three hundred years, and the sound arrives after the light, and the light is not fire, it is the sky being replaced. The ash comes down like snow that has made up its mind. The river stands up. And in the gray, one sound runs the whole length of the colony — not screaming, not yet — the bell, the church bell, ringing by itself again, and this time someone is ringing it back."

Then the night, and the morning, and the reckoning: the tracks resolve — every number the table kept, spent; every number they let fall, collected. And after the ash settles, the charter: who holds the paper now, and what it means, and the three epilogues — the free town upstream, singing the amended song; the Company town on the coast, rebuilt, owned, alive; the scattered people, forty-one boats' worth, carrying the colony the way the River Folk carry the song-cycle. Give each player the last line of their officer's epilogue. The ledger closes. The colony, in whichever shape the table chose, remains.

Chapter ElevenRunning Management as Drama

The golden rules, in order of importance. One: every number has a face — when Food drops, Gran Meera knocks; when Defense drops, Holt's rounds get longer; when Morale drops, the tavern's lamp goes out early. If a track moves and no one knocks, you have done bookkeeping, not drama. Two: every surplus has a claimant — a good harvest is not a solved problem, it is four petitions in a queue. Three: never let a phase be arithmetic — the ledger is read aloud in ninety seconds, and then the doors open. Four: scarcity is the story's engine, but never let scarcity become stasis — a colony that is only surviving is a colony the players are only visiting; give every shortage an expedition inside it. Five: the Company must be reasonable — Penhale's quotas have reasons, Fallow's audit has law, and the offer in Season Four has ships; a reasonable antagonist is a mirror, and an unreasonable one is weather. Six: the cult is not wrong about the mountain — Kell's data is good, his certainty is the wound; let the players discover that the madman was the only one who did the reading, and sit with the discomfort. Seven: failure is a fork, never an ending — a lost harvest is the River Folk's offer; a burned charter is the free town; the colony can be broken in four different directions and still be a story. Eight: the players' arguments are the product — when the council splits three-two over the weir-rights at midnight, pour the tea and let it run; that argument is the game.

Pacing. One session per season is the frame; take two for any season the table is invested in — Season Four will ask for two by itself. Between sessions, the colony does not pause: send the players a one-paragraph "letter from the clerk" — the weather, the tally, one rumor — and the next session opens warm.

Adaptation. The framework travels: a sci-fi colony under a failing terraformer, a generation ship's council, a fantasy free-town under a dragon's mountain — swap the volcano, keep the ledger, keep the factions, keep the turn. The genre's secret is that the arithmetic was never the point. The point is forty-one people, a door that knocks, and the table arguing about grain as if it were gold — because, on the River Cinder, it is.

Chapter OneWhat This Tome Is

This tome is a complete social-investigation and slice-of-life romance adventure for two to five players, written for the game master. It runs three to four sessions and is set within the prestigious, gaslit halls of the Lumina Conservatory—an ancient academy of arts, sciences, and secret histories nestled high above a misty lake town. The characters are student novices, not battle-hardened mercenaries, and the adventure is designed so that the most devastating strikes are delivered through a perfectly timed rumor, an exquisite waltz, or a quiet confession under the greenhouse glass.

The campaign spans four terms of a single academic year: Induction, Frost-Fair, Spring-Gala, and the Finals. It is completely system-agnostic; wherever a social tension is tested, translate the stakes to whatever charisma, leverage, or observation check your ruleset prefers. Use the stats sketches to translate individual student rivalries into mechanical terms freely.

The tone to aim for is whimsical but sharp. While the surface is all library dust, stolen notes, and tea garden gossip, there is real weight to the choices. Romancing a rival or siding with an outcast professor has consequences that ripple through their grades, their social standing, and ultimately, the survival of the academy itself. Do not run this as a parody; treat the students' passions, heartbreaks, and petty feuds with absolute dignity. When a heart breaks, let the rain against the stained-glass windows feel loud.

Chapter TwoThe Academic Secret

Warden's Margin

This chapter is the secret foundation of the Conservatory, which the players will uncover as they win hearts and read old archives.

Fifty years ago, the Conservatory's founder, Arch-Chancellor Theron, did not build Lumina's grand floating observatory through architectural genius alone. He bargained with an ancient, localized spirit of the mists known as the Loom-Steward. In exchange for the genius to elevate Lumina above its academic rivals, Theron bound the school's social balance to the Steward's engine: a hidden clockwork loom deep beneath the under-vaults that runs on the emotional resonance of the academy's residents. High-intensity bonds—fervent love, deep-seated rivalries, desperate ambition—literally spin the silver thread that keeps the observatory afloat.

This balance is currently failing because the current Arch-Chancellor, Madam Vane, is attempting to cold-press the school into a sterile, hyper-standardized curriculum to appease the external High Council. She has banned social societies, restricted the Spring Gala, and systematically discouraged close personal attachments among students. By suppressing these bonds, Vane is starvation-dieting the Loom-Steward. The silver threads are snapping: the observatory has begun to list by half a degree each month, tremors shake the lecture halls, and the mists are rising to swallow the lower gardens.

The players are caught in this delicate web. To save the Conservatory, they must either defy Vane's sterile curriculum by actively cultivating intense emotional bonds among the student body (spinning the silver threads to full strength), or help Vane safely dismantle the Loom-Steward's engine forever—which will ground the floating observatory, strip the academy of its legendary status, but finally free the student body from being unwitting fuel for Theron's ancient bargain. Your final session will be decided by the relationships they have forged and which destiny they choose for Lumina.

Chapter ThreeThe Social Engine

Running a romance and gossip-heavy campaign requires a different set of GM tools than a dungeon crawl. This chapter details the three mechanics that drive the adventure's pace and tension: the Gossip Track, the Bond Levels, and the Academic Strain.

The Gossip Track. The academy is a whispering gallery. The Gossip Track represents the current rumors swirling around the players, rated from zero to ten. At zero, they are social ghosts, completely ignored by the major cliques. At ten, every glance is scrutinized, a single spilled teacup is treated as a scandal, and Vane's disciplinary monitors are actively shadow-following them. Gossip is gained by public displays of affection, failed social checks, and high-stakes rivalries. It can be spent to buy invitations to exclusive salons, forge alibis, or discredit a rival during class presentations.

Bond Levels (1 to 5). Every major NPC student has a Bond Level with each player, starting at Level 1 (Casual Acquaintance) and peaking at Level 5 (Soul Bound or Arch-Rival). A player cannot simply roll a die to increase a Bond. It requires a Bond Scene: a distinct, quiet interaction that takes place after hours—a midnight study session, a shared umbrella in the rain, or a confrontation in the restricted section. If a Bond is Level 5, that NPC will stand beside the player in the final confrontation against Vane, bringing their unique family influence, academic talent, or physical wardenship to bear.

Academic Strain. Every player has an Academic Strain track (0 to 5) representing their coursework. Every social outing, midnight tryst, or investigation into the school's under-vaults increases Strain. If Strain hits 5, the player is placed on Academic Probation: they lose their weekend privileges, cannot attend the Gala, and receive a severe penalty to all social checks with students who fear being associated with a failure. Strain is lowered by spending the afternoon studying in the library, passing term exams, or getting a rival to write their essay for them.

Chapter FourThe Cast

Julian Thorne, the brooding scholar. Nineteen, heir to a dying estate, walks with a slight limp and carries a notebook he shields from sight with a writer's paranoia. Julian is the brilliant, melancholy top of the class, specializing in forbidden linguistics. He acts cold and dismissive of the player's presence, but it is a defense: his family is deeply indebted to Lady Vexley, and he has been ordered to spy on the school's archives. Play him as intensely articulate, prone to staring out wet windows, and secretly desperate for someone to talk to about poetry without laughing. Bond bonus: Julian's library pass grants unlimited access to the Restricted Section, lowering Academic Strain.

Cynthia Vane, the Arch-Chancellor's daughter. Eighteen, perfect posture, dresses in pristine Lumina white, and is the absolute arbiter of social status in the dining hall. Cynthia is her mother's perfect instrument—until she isn't. She is secretly a talented painter, a forbidden art her mother considers a waste of intellectual capital. If the player discovers her hidden studio in the attic of the old observatory, her perfect facade cracks. Play her as sharp, impeccably polite, and wielding social exclusion like a rapier. Bond bonus: Cynthia can raise or lower any player's Gossip Track by 3 points at will.

Alistair "Pip" Montgomery, the scoundrel with a heart of gold. Seventeen, third son of a minor baron, has never read a textbook he couldn't turn into a paper glider. Alistair is the school's premier smuggler of contraband—foreign tea, forbidden novels, and vintage wine. He knows every secret staircase and loose floorboard in the Conservatory. Play him with a permanent smirk, a pockets-full-of-treats energy, and an absolute dread of being taken seriously. Bond bonus: Alistair can acquire any item from the town below within twelve hours, bypassing Vane's security.

Professor Evelyn Thorne, the eccentric history mistress. Master Elias Thorne's younger sister, forty, with ink-stained fingers and glasses she constantly misplaces on her own head. Evelyn knows that Theron's old diaries are missing and suspects her brother's clockwork methods were inspired by Lumina's under-vaults. She is your historical anchor and leak: win her trust, and she will let the players into the under-vaults during the winter solstice. Stat sketch: high academic knowledge, completely disorganized, will write glowing letters of recommendation if bribed with rare teas.

The Loom-Steward. The ancient place-spirit bound to the school's foundation. It has no physical form, but it speaks through the ticking of the observatory's great clock, the rustle of dry leaves in the courtyard, and the silver threads that spin in the under-vaults. When it communicates, its voice is a choir of whispers that repeat the player's own secret thoughts back to them: "We spun your first love into silver. We can spin your betrayal into iron. Do not let us starve." It is neither good nor evil; it is a creature of pure sentiment, and it wants the school to stay passionate, no matter how messy the passion is.

Chapter FiveThe Lumina Conservatory

The Conservatory is an architectural marvel of high-arched stone, gaslit corridors, and floating structures. The Grand Quad: the central courtyard of white stone where students gather between lectures, with Theron's statue at its heart—its bronze hand is always cold, and tradition says touching it before an exam brings luck. The floating observatory: Lumina's crowning glory, suspended three hundred feet above the lake by the Loom-Steward's silver threads. It houses the legendary telescope and the school's most exclusive study alcoves.

The glasshouses, on the southern slope: a tropical wonderland under iron frames, smelling of damp earth and orchids, where the air is always warm even during the winter Frost-Fair. It is the school's premier location for late-night trysts, quiet confessions, and suspicious rendezvous. The archives: a multi-tiered labyrinth of cedar shelves, managed by a clockwork librarian that files books by weight rather than subject—making a search for historical secrets a literal puzzle of balance. And the under-vaults: the dark, wet foundation of the school, where the mists pool thickest and the Loom-Steward's engine clicks and spins its silver thread in the dark.

Sensory details define the Conservatory: the smell of rain-wet stone, old paper, and hot chocolate in the dining hall; the sound of distant piano practice, the constant clicking of the observatory's gears, and the hollow wind coming off the mist-lake. Make these details feel intimate—the touch of a damp wool coat, the scratch of a steel pen, the warmth of a shared lantern on a cold walk. These are the details that turn a social game into an immersive reality.

Chapter SixAct One — The Induction & The Gossip

The first act is the Induction term, and its job is to set the social board, introduce the rivals, and hook the players into Lumina's whisper-fueled economy. Structure it around three key events.

The Chancellor's welcome. As the players sit in the Great Hall under the hanging observatory, read this aloud:

Read Aloud

"The Great Hall smells of fresh beeswax and anxiety. Above you, the floating observatory hangs like a giant brass pear, its shadow crawling across the rows of new novices. At the high podium, Madam Vane looks down at you through steel-rimmed spectacles. 'You are here to learn discipline, order, and the quiet pursuit of truth,' she says, her voice dry as parchment. 'The passions of youth are a distraction we do not permit here. Focus on your slates, forget your attachments, and keep your gaze forward.' Above her, the great brass pear shudders, a single degree off-center, and a drop of silver grease falls silently into her water glass."

The first salon. In the evening, the novices gather in the conservatory lounge. This is your social sandbox: let the players meet Julian, Cynthia, and Pip. Ask each player to spend one scene attempting to make an impression on one of them—using wit, elegance, or shared secrets. Let them learn the first rumors: Julian is writing a poem about someone in the room; Cynthia's mother has a key that goes nowhere; Pip is looking for someone to help him steal the exam keys from the faculty lounge.

The first leak. End the session with a midnight encounter. A player, sneaking out after hours to avoid Academic Strain, meets Professor Evelyn in the glasshouses. She is looking for her lost notebook, but she has found something else: a silver thread, thick as a rope, growing out of an orchid pot and vibrating on a C-sharp note. She tells the player, quietly: "The school is losing its key. If Vane tightens the screws any further, we are all going to fall into the lake." The session ends with the player holding the silver thread and feeling the pulse of the school's heart beneath their palm.

Chapter SevenAct Two — The Frost-Fair & The First Bond

Act Two is the winter term, and its theme is the Frost-Fair—a week-long carnival on the frozen lake where the school's social rules are temporarily suspended. The structure is open: the players must balance their studies (lowering Academic Strain) with the high-stakes opportunities of the fair to raise their Bond Levels with their chosen targets.

The ice-waltz. The center of the Frost-Fair is the Lake Pavilion, lit by paper lanterns. The players can invite Julian, Cynthia, or Pip to dance. Run this as a waltz scene, not a single roll: the player must navigate three phases of the dance—the Approach, the Turn, and the Whisper—using different skills. A success raises their Bond Level to 3 (Intimate Companion); a failure raises their Gossip Track by two as the school watches them slip on the ice together.

The library audit. While the fair is loudest, Madam Vane initiates a surprise audit of student lockers, looking for contraband. Pip needs the players to help him move his entire inventory of imported wine and forbidden letters from the boathouse to the observatory's attic. This is a high-stakes stealth mission through the misty quad: if they succeed, Pip's Bond Level climbs, and they gain a permanent smuggling contact; if they fail, they face Vane in her office, and the Academic Strain track ticks to 4.

The solstice tremor. On the night of the winter solstice, the lake ice cracks with a sound like a gunshot. The observatory above the quad drops three feet in a single breath, sending the faculty into a panic. The players, if they have won Professor Evelyn's trust, are guided down into the under-vaults for the first time. They see the silver loom: its gears are choked with black dust, and the silver thread is spinning thin and frayed. The Loom-Steward whispers to them from the dark of the vault: "Bring us the warmth we are owed, or the winter will never end." They realize that their growing bonds—and their growing rivalries—are the only thing keeping the observatory from crashing into the ice.

Chapter EightAct Three — The Spring-Gala & The Choice

Act Three is the spring term, and everything reaches its boiling point. Madam Vane, terrified by the winter solstice tremor, announces that the annual Spring Gala is canceled, and all social societies are officially disbanded. This is the moment of rebellion: the players must organize an illegal, underground Gala in the old glasshouse, bring the student body together under Vane's nose, and force the final choice.

Organizing the underground Gala. The players must use their Bond Levels and Gossip Track to secure three things: Cynthia's help to forge the invitations, Pip's contacts to smuggle in the punch, and Julian's translation of the old school anthem—the waltz that Theron used fifty years ago to tune the Loom-Steward. Run this as a sequence of high-pressure social scenes where every player must call in their favors.

The Gala scene. The night of the Gala in the glasshouses is your campaign's emotional climax. Read this aloud as the music starts under the orchids:

Read Aloud

"The glasshouses are ablaze with stolen candles, their light reflecting off the wet glass like a sky of fallen stars. The music—Julian's translated waltz, played on a smuggled violin—takes the room, and for a moment, Lumina is what it was always meant to be: beautiful, passionate, and dangerously alive. Cynthia is dancing with Alistair; Julian is watching you from the shadows of the ferns, his notebook forgotten in his hand. Below your feet, the silver loom begins to sing. The floor under you rises, gently, steadily, as the silver thread spins thick as iron. You are afloat. You are free. And then, the door opens, and Madam Vane stands in the rain at the threshold."

The confrontation. Vane arrives not with guards, but with a document: the High Council's charter to dismantle the observatory safely and move the school to the mainland. She presents her argument: the Loom-Steward is an emotional parasite, feeding on the students' pain, their heartbreaks, and their rivalries for fifty years to sustain Theron's vanity. She asks the players to help her safely unwind the loom's mainspring before the finals next week. The choice is set: support Vane and help her dismantle the loom—the academy is grounded, the magic is gone, but the students are free of the Steward's hunger; or defy Vane and lead the student body in a full strike—renewing Theron's bargain, keeping Lumina in the sky, and sealing the players' own bonds into the school's silver foundation forever. Let the table argue; the future of the academy rests on who they love.

Chapter TenThe Graduation & Epilogues

The campaign ends at the Graduation Ceremony, one year after the Induction, under a sky that is either empty of the observatory or shimmering with silver threads.

The Grounded Ending (Vane's path). The observatory is dismantled. Lumina is now a stone academy on the lake shore, quiet, disciplined, and ordinary. The Floating Ending (Rebellion path). The observatory remains in the sky, a glittering jewel held by silver threads thick as tree roots. The players are the new senior wardens of Lumina; Vane is exiled; Julian's translated waltz is played at every morning assembly; and the Loom-Steward sings in the vaults, fed by a new generation of love and rivalries. Last lines. Give each player the last line of their character's diary entry on graduation night. Let them define what their relationship meant and whether the year's whispers were worth the silence that follows.

Chapter ElevenRunning Romance & Gossip

Keep the focus on the NPCs. Lumina is only as interesting as its residents. Julian, Cynthia, and Pip must feel like real people with their own fears, ambitions, and schedules. They do not wait for the players to initiate; they send notes, they sit at their tables, they have their own arguments. If Cynthia is angry at Julian, the players are forced to take sides in the dining hall.

Fail-forward social rolls. A failed social roll must never mean "nothing happens." If a player fails to charm Julian, he doesn't just ignore them; he is offended, or he writes a biting epigram about them in his notebook, or Cynthia notices and uses it to tease them. Social friction is the engine of the game.

The power of whispers. Use the Gossip Track as a literal economy. If a player wants to know what Julian is hiding, they don't just search his room; they trade a rumor about Alistair's latest smuggling run to a second-year student who has the library keys. It makes the school feel like a living, breathing community where every word is a currency.

Last counsel. This tome is about what we are willing to pay for wonder: is a magical school worth the emotional fuel it consumes? Let the players fall in love with Lumina, let them fall in love with its rivals, and when the final choice arrives, let them discover that the most powerful magic at the table was the connection they forged when they weren't rolling dice.
